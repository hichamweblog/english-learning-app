Lesson 20 – Determiners 
Today’s lesson focuses on determiners – words that describe quantity, such as 
some, any, much, many, few, little, all, every, and each. These words are used 
quite frequently in English, so it’s important to use them correctly! 
some & any 
Which of these four sentences is incorrect? 
• I don’t have a car. 
• I don’t have any car. 
• I don’t have any pets. 
• I don’t have any experience. 
The incorrect sentence is “I don’t have any car” – it should be “I don’t have a car.” 
Why? Because we can use some and any with plural nouns and uncountable nouns 
– NOT with singular countable nouns. With singular countable nouns, we use a/an 
instead. 
Here’s an example with some – try to identify the incorrect sentence: 
• We have an idea. 
• We have some advice. 
• We have some suggestions. 
• We have some comment. 
The incorrect sentence is “We have some comment” – again, because “comment” is a 
singular, countable noun. We could say “We have a comment” or “We have some 
comments” – both of these would be correct. 
Now, when do we use some, and when do we use any? 
We usually use some in positive statements, and any in negative statements and 
questions: 
• I got some feedback from the teacher. (positive) 
• I didn’t get any feedback from the teacher. (negative) 
• I never get any feedback from the teacher. (negative) 
• Nobody in the class gave me any feedback. (negative) 
• None of the students got any feedback. (negative) 


• Did you get any feedback? (question) 
Exceptions: 
• We can use some in a question that makes an offer: “Would you like some 
mashed potatoes?” 
• We can also use some in a question where we expect the answer to be “yes.” 
These questions often start with a negative: “Don’t you have some money 
saved up?” 
• We can use any even if there’s no negative word in the sentence with the 
words barely, hardly, rarely, scarcely, and seldom: “They look the same. 
There’s hardly any difference between them.” 
The word some has a couple of special uses: 
• It can be used to describe an approximate number:  
o Some twenty percent of the population is unemployed. 
o The castle is located some five miles outside the city.  
(We could also say “About twenty percent” and “about five miles”) 
• Some can be used with a singular noun to emphasize the fact that the noun is 
unknown and unimportant:  
o “Some guy kept following me around the club and asking for my phone 
number.”  
o “The internet’s down. There must be some problem with the 
network.”  
(We could also say a guy or a problem – but the use of the word “some” 
emphasizes the fact that “I don’t know / don’t care.”) 
If we have recently mentioned the noun, we can use some and any alone, without 
the noun after it: 
• I just bought ice cream. Do you want some? (= some ice cream) 
• “Mangoes are on sale!” 
“Yeah, I’m going to buy some!” (= some mangoes) 
• There were supposed to be fireworks during the parade, but I didn’t see any. 
(= any fireworks) 
• That leftover pasta we brought home from the restaurant – is there any left? 
(= any pasta) 
 


much, many, a lot of, lots of 
We use much with uncountable nouns, and many with countable nouns: 
• I spent too much money on vacation. 
• I bought too many souvenirs. 
You can remember this because the word much has a u in it, u for “uncountable.” 
We use more (when comparing two things) and most (when comparing three or 
more things) and these can be used with all nouns: 
• I spent more money than you did. 
• I bought more souvenirs than you did. 
• I spent the most money out of everyone in my family. 
• I bought the most souvenirs out of everyone in my family. 
In everyday spoken English, we usually use the expression a lot of or lots of instead 
of much/many to refer to a large quantity. These can be used with all nouns: 
• Much information is available on this topic. 
• A lot of / Lots of information is available on this topic. 
• Many books have been written about this. 
• A lot of / Lots of books have been written about this. 
The only exception is with time – we say many days/weeks/months/years, not “a 
lot of” or “lots of” days/weeks/etc. 
Like some and any, we can use much and many without a noun following it, if we 
have recently mentioned that noun: 
• He’s made a lot of progress, but I haven’t made much. (=much progress) 
• The employees are worried. Many are saying that pay cuts are coming. 
(=many employees) 
few, little, less, fewer 
Can you identify the two correct sentences and the two incorrect sentences? 
• There’s been few publicity about this issue. 
• There’s been little publicity about this issue. 
• I have a few things to say about it. 


• I have a little things to say about it. 
The incorrect sentences are: 
• There’s been few publicity about this issue. 
• I have a little things to say about it. 
We use few, fewer, and fewest with countable nouns, and little, less, and least 
with uncountable nouns. 
• The finance department has fewer employees than human resources. 
• The marketing department has the fewest employees in the company. 
• The local roads have less traffic than the highway. 
• This road has the least traffic of all.

There’s actually a slight difference between saying few and a few, and little and a 
little. A few and a little have a more positive connotation, and few and little have a 
more negative connotation. 
• I have a few friends = good 
• I have few friends = bad 
• I have a little money = good 
• I have little money = bad 
Exception:  
Don’t be fooled by the expression "quite a few" – this actually means “a lot”! For 
example – “Quite a few people came to the show. The tickets were nearly sold out.” 
Like the other determiners, we can use a few and a little alone if we have recently 
mentioned the noun they refer to: 
• “I don’t have any paper clips.” 
“I’ve got a few – here you go.” (= a few paper clips) 
• “Did we use up all the butter?” 
“No, there’s still a little left.” (= a little butter) 


In informal everyday English, we often say not much or not many:  
• I don’t have much time. (more common) 
• I have little time. (sounds formal) 
• There aren’t very many chips in this bag. (more common) 
• There are few chips in this bag. (less common) 
For uncountable nouns, we can also say a bit of or a little bit of: 
• I like to put a bit of cinnamon in my coffee. 
• We’ve still got a little bit of work to do. 
 
all, whole, every, each 
Can you identify the incorrect sentence among these three? 
• All the class enjoyed the lesson. 
• All the books are new. 
• All the coffee we sell is organic. 
We use all with plural nouns and uncountable nouns – so the second and third 
sentences are OK, but the first sentence is incorrect. With singular, countable nouns, 
we usually use whole or entire instead: 
• The whole/entire class enjoyed the lesson. 
There is one exception – you can say all day, all week, all month, or all year to 
mean “the entire day/week/month/year.” There’s a difference between all day and 
every day: 
• all day = 100% of one day, from the morning to the evening 
(length of time) 
• every day = Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, 
Sunday (frequency) 
With uncountable or plural nouns, we can say either all or all of – they’re the same: 
• I finished all my homework. 
• I finished all of my homework. 
• I greeted all the students. 


• I greeted all of the students. 
However, after all of, we need to have the word the, this, that, these, those, or a 
possessive (my, your, his, her, etc.) – we cannot say “all of + noun”: 
• I finished all of homework. 
• I greeted all of students. 
• All of clothes are clean. 
• All of these clothes are clean. 
• All of the clothes are clean. 
• All of my clothes are clean. 
Now let’s look at every and each. In many cases they are the same: 
• I put a label on each bottle. 
• I put a label on every bottle. 
There are some situations where we choose one or the other: 
• With the words almost, nearly, and virtually, use every: 
I got nearly every question right. 
• With frequency numbers, use every: 
I go to the dentist every six months. 
• We have some fixed expressions with every: 
We have a meeting every other week. (= one week yes, one week no) 
Every single member of the team is important. (= emphasizes individuality) 
I go to the beach every so often / every once in a while. (=occasionally) 
• If you want to talk about two individual things in a pair, use each: 
I have two suitcases, and each weighs more than 20 kg. 
• You can say every + noun and each + noun or each of the + noun, but not 
every of + noun: 
Each employee has been trained. 
Each of the employees has been trained. 
Every employee has been trained. 
Every of employees has been trained.
