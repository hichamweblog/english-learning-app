Lesson 39: Participle Phrases 
Yesterday you learned that information can be added to a sentence by using a 
phrase beginning with a present participle (ING form) or past participle (ED form 
or irregular): 
• Hoping to get an interview, Ryan sent his resume to the company. 
• Performed properly, this strategy is very effective. 
• Not wanting to wake her, I closed the door carefully. 
(with –ING forms, we can also add the word “not” at the beginning) 
These phrases tell us information about the timing of an event, its causes or results, 
or a condition. Today’s lesson will cover phrases with: 
• having + past participle 
• prepositions + participle phrases 
• clauses without the verb “to be” 
Having + past participle 
We use this structure if the action in the main clause is a consequence of the event in 
the participle clause. 
• Having finished all my work, I decided to go home. 
               (reason/cause) 
 
 
(result) 
• Having bought a big bag of chips, we felt obligated to eat them all. 
               (reason/cause) 
 
 
 
(result) 
• Having lived here for years, Paul knows the city very well. 
             (reason/cause) 
 
 
(result) 
We can also reverse the order of the sentence and put the participle phrase at the 
end: 
• Paul knows the city very well, having lived there for years. 
Now, in the previous lesson you learned that the participle phrase must be near the 
person/thing it describes – so does this sentence break the rule? 
The answer is no, because the phrase still describes the subject of the main clause – 
“Paul.” In other words, there’s a match between the subject and the participle 
phrase. 


This would be an incorrect (or at least confusing) sentence: 
• I'll ask Paul about the city, having lived there for years. 
The subject of the main clause is “I,” but the participle phrase describes “Paul” – 
there’s a mismatch. Here’s another example of an incorrect sentence: 
• Having parked the car, the children unbuckled their seatbelts. 
This makes it sound like the children were driving the car! 
• Having parked the car, the mother told her children they could unbuckle 
their seatbelts. This sentence makes it clear that the mother was driving. 
The participle phrase must describe the main subject of the main clause. 
Another structure is having + been + past participle, which is used when the 
original sentences are in the passive voice structure. 
• I was offered a great salary. I couldn’t refuse it.  
= Having been offered a great salary, I couldn’t refuse it. 
• We were fooled once before. We were reluctant to trust the salesman. 
= Having been fooled once before, we were reluctant to trust the salesman. 
 
Prepositions + Participle Phrases 
We can also start participle phrases with these common prepositions:  
• Time: after, before, while, since  
• Reason/Result/Condition: by, with, without 
Here are some examples: 
• After dropping my kids off at school, I went to the grocery store. 
• Before starting the exam, you should read the instructions carefully. 
• He broke his leg while playing football. 
• Since appearing on TV, I’ve gotten a lot of phone calls. 
Avoid this common error – using the base form of the verb after the preposition: 
• After drop my kids off 
• Before start the exam 
There are two possible correct forms – but you can’t use just the base form. 


• After dropping my kids off 
• After I dropped my kids off 
• Before starting the exam 
• Before you start the exam 
Here are some examples using by, with, and without: 
• By writing every day, he was able to finish his book in a year. 
They survived in the forest by eating wild berries. 
• With time running out, we had to make some quick decisions. 
I can’t concentrate with the kids making so much noise. 
• Without knowing all the details, I can’t give you any advice. 
He recited the entire poem without making a single mistake. 
Again, avoid the error of using the base form. In fact, when using by, with, and 
without, the only correct option is the –ING form: 
• without know all the details 
• without I know all the details 
• by eat wild berries 
• by they eat wild berries 
Clauses without the verb “to be” 
When the verb in the clause is “to be,” we can sometimes omit it and begin the 
clause with a conjunction or adjective: 
• While in New York, we visited the Statue of Liberty. 
= While we were in New York 
• Whenever possible, I try to get along with everybody. 
= Whenever it is possible 
• Although shocked, she managed to speak firmly. 
= Although she was shocked 
• Finally satisfied with the price, he made the purchase. 
= Finally being satisfied with the price, 
• The community, angry about the injustice, created a petition. 
= which was angry about the injustice 
 


Summary 
• Use having + past participle if the action in the main clause is a consequence 
of the event in the participle clause. 
o Having finished all my work, I decided to go home. 
• We can also reverse the order of the sentence and put the participle phrase 
at the end - as long as the participle phrase matches the subject of the 
main clause. 
o Paul knows the city very well, having lived there for years. 
• Another structure is having + been + past participle, which is used when we 
have the passive voice. 
o Having been offered a great salary, I couldn’t refuse it. 
• We can use prepositions to begin participle clauses: 
o After dropping my kids off at school, I went to the grocery store. 
o By writing every day, he was able to finish his book in a year. 
• Avoid the common error of using the base form of the verb after the 
preposition. 
• When the verb in the clause is "to be," we can sometimes omit it and begin 
the clause with a conjunction or adjective: 
o While in New York, we visited the Statue of Liberty. 
= While we were in New York 
o Finally satisfied with the price, he made the purchase. 
= Finally being satisfied with the price,
