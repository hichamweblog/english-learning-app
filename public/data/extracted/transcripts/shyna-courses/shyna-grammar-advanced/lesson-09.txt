Lesson 9 – Modal Verbs (Part 2) 
Welcome to part 2 of our lesson on modal verbs - today you'll learn modals for 
requests, advice, offers, and asking permission. 
Modal Verbs for Requests 
When asking somebody else to do something, we have three options: 
Can you help me with my homework? 
more informal 
Could you help me with my homework? 
more formal / polite 
Would you mind helping me with my 
homework? 
extremely polite 
The third option, "would you mind," is usually used when we are concerned that our 
request might inconvenience the other person, or we suspect that the other person 
might say "no" or have some objection. 
Notice that after can and could, we use the base form of the verb ("help") - but 
after would you mind, we use the -ING form ("helping"). 
Put it into practice! 
Imagine you have an assistant who can help you with any task in your professional 
or personal life. Ask your assistant to do three different things for you: 
• 
Can you...? 
• 
Could you...? 
• 
Would you mind...? 
Modal Verbs for Advice 
When asking for advice, we use should: 


• Question word + should: 
This is a serious problem. What should I do? 
Where should we go on our vacation? 
 
• Should I do A or B? 
Should I go to grad school, or just try to get a job? 
Should I cut my hair or let it grow? 
Should we buy this or not? 
 
• Do you think I should... [action]? 
Do you think I should tell him how I really feel? 
It seems like a good job. Do you think I should take it? 
Put it into practice! 
Imagine you are talking with a very intelligent and wise person. Think of three 
situations in your life on which you would like to receive some advice, and ask this 
person for their advice: 
• 
What should I do about... ? 
• 
Should I ________ or _________? 
• 
Do you think I should... ? 
When giving advice, we can use should (for giving a strong recommendation) 
or could (for simply suggesting a possibility). 
Suggesting possibilities: 
I just moved here, and I don't have any friends. 
What should I do? 
Well, you could join a club or sports team. Or if 
sports isn't your thing, you could get involved at the 
local community center. You could also throw a 
party at your house and invite all your new 
neighbors. 
Giving a strong recommendation: 


I've had a stomachache for the past week. What kind of medicine do you 
think I should take? 
You should go to the doctor - it might be something serious! 
Two more expressions used for giving strong advice (which can be used as 
substitutes for "should") are had better and ought to. 
Ought to is often used to express some sort of duty, moral obligation, or "rightness": 
• That was a terrible thing you did. You ought to apologize. 
• He's an adult! He ought to know to behave by now. 
Had better is used for strong recommendations and warnings: 
• She's not breathing. We'd better call an ambulance, quickly! 
• You've had a lot to drink. Give me your car 
keys; you'd better take a taxi. 
• It's raining really hard. I'd better take an umbrella. 
(making a recommendation to yourself) 
• Hey man, you'd better stay away from my 
girlfriend! (warning) 
• They said they'd meet us here at 6:30. They'd 
better not be late, otherwise we'll miss the 
beginning of the show. 
Put it into practice! 
Imagine that four people are asking you for advice. Say one sentence to respond to 
each of their problems: 
1. My job pays well, but it's kind of boring. What should I do? 
You should... ____________________________________________ 
2. My kids don't get along with each other - they're always fighting. Got any 
ideas? 
You could... ____________________________________________ 


3. I just found out that my best friend's husband is cheating on her. What should 
I do? 
You ought to... ____________________________________________ 
4. I have $20,000 of credit card debt and it just keeps going up every month. 
You'd better... ____________________________________________ 
Modal Verbs for Offers 
We often use Would you like...? to make a 
polite offer or invitation to another person: 
• 
Would you like something to drink? 
• 
Would you like to join me for lunch? 
• 
Would you like me to help you with 
that project? 
This is a more polite way of saying "Do you want...?" - which could be used among 
friends or in informal situations. 
When talking about something that you want, saying "I'd like..." (short for "I would 
like") is a more polite way of saying "I want." This is a good way to express your 
desires in a polite way; sometimes "I want" can be a little too direct - especially 
when what you want involves another person. 
• 
I'd like you to make this project your top priority. 
• 
I'd like to reschedule our meeting, if that's all right with you. 
• 
I'd like us to plan ahead and stay organized. 
Put it into practice! 
An exchange student from another country has just arrived at your house; he will be 
staying with you for several months and he knows nothing about your country. 
Make him some offers and invitations using: 
• 
(offer a specific food/drink):  
Would you like... ? 
• 
(invite him to go somewhere in your city):  
Would you like... ? 


• 
(offer to let him borrow a possession of yours):  
Would you like... ? 
Modal Verbs for Asking Permission 
When asking permission, we have at least seven different options: 
• 
Can I leave work early today? 
• 
Could I leave work early today? 
• 
May I leave work early today? 
• 
Do you mind if I leave work early today? 
• 
Would you mind if I left work early today? 
• 
Is it OK if I leave work early today? 
• 
Would it be all right if I left work early today? 
Asking permission with can is very informal. We usually 
use this with friends: 
• 
Hey, can I borrow your cell phone for a minute? 
Asking permission with could is more polite. We can use this with friends or in 
professional situations: 
• 
Could I have some more coffee, please? 
• 
Could I use the conference room for my meeting this afternoon? 
Asking permission with may is also very polite. Nowadays, it is less commonly used 
than could: 
• 
May I use your bathroom? 
• 
May I go ahead with this project? 
Finally, we ask permission with Do you mind if I... or Would you mind if I... when 
we are concerned that our request might inconvenience the other person, or we 
suspect that the other person might say "no" or have some objection. If your boss 
says: 
• 
I'd like you to call our supplier today. 


• 
Actually, would you mind if I called them tomorrow? I already have a 
number of urgent things to deal with. 
• 
Actually, do you mind if I call them tomorrow? 
Notice that when we use "would you mind," the verb is in the past tense ("called") 
and when we use "do you mind," the verb is in the present tense ("call"). 
It is the same with the phrases "Is it OK if..." and "Would it be all 
right if..." 
• 
Is it OK if I take these textbooks home? I'll return them 
tomorrow. 
• 
Would it be all right if I took these textbooks home? I'll 
return them tomorrow. 
Finally, to respond to requests for permission beginning with can, 
could, may, Is it OK, and Would it be all right...? you can say 
"Yes" or "Sure" or "Go right ahead!" 
However, to respond "Yes" to the questions beginning with "Do you 
mind" and "Would you mind," you can say "That's fine" or "No, not at all" - that 
means you are giving permission; you have no objections! 
Put it into practice! 
Ask a question requesting permission in each of these four situations: 
1. You're at your friend's house and the window is open, but you are cold. 
Can I... ____________________________________________? 
2. Your final exam is scheduled for Friday, but you can't come to class on that 
day. 
Could I... ____________________________________________? 
3. You're at a restaurant with a colleague, and the grilled salmon they ordered 
looks delicious; you want to taste it. 
May I... ____________________________________________? 
4. At work, your computer is broken and you really need to write some e-mails. 
You ask your co-worker... 
Would you mind if I... ____________________________________________? 


Summary 
• 
To ask someone else to do something, use: 
o can you, could you, would you mind? 
• 
To ask for advice, use should 
• 
To give advice, use:  
o should (for recommendations)  
o could (for possibilities)  
o ought to (for doing what is right)  
o had better (for very strong recommendations and warnings) 
• 
To make polite offers, use Would you like...? 
• 
To express your desires politely, use I'd like rather than "I want" 
• 
To ask permission, use:  
o can I (less formal) 
o could I 
o may I 
o do you mind if I / would you mind if I...? 
o Is it OK if I... and Would it be all right if I...?  
• 
With the "would" questions, use the simple past tense of the verb; with all 
others use the simple present tense
