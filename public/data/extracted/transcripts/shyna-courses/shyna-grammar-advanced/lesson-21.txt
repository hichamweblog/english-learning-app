Lesson 21: Possessives 
Let’s start by reviewing the basic possessives in English, and then I’ll explain some 
of the complicated and confusing cases. 
Basic Possessives 
Use my before a noun, and mine when the possessive is by itself: 
• That’s my book. 
• That book is mine.

Beware of this common error: its and it’s. 
In English, we use the apostrophe (‘)  
• to indicate the short forms of words: (he’s = he is, don’t = do not) 
• for possessives: (John’s wife, my mother’s clothes, the company’s profits) 
…and this is what causes the confusion.  
• It’s always means “it is” 


• Its always means the possessive 
You can remember this by imagining the apostrophe as a tiny little letter “i” – so 
“it’s” is short for “it is.” 
And in the word its, the “s” is closer to the “t” – when you possess something, you 
want to keep it close to you. So its is always used to indicate possession.

Confusing Case #1 
What if the noun is two words? 
We put the ‘s only after the second word: 
• the vice-president’s speech 
• the assistant director’s career 
• my hair dryer’s cord 
Confusing Case #2 
What’s the difference between these two expressions? 
• my brother’s friends 
• my brothers’ friends 
In the first case, I only have one brother. In the second case, I have two or more 
brothers. To make the possessive of a plural noun ending in S, we simply add an 
apostrophe without an extra ‘s: 
• my parents’ house 
• the students’ textbooks 


For irregular plural nouns (those not ending in S), then we do include the ‘s: 
• the children’s toys 
• the children’ toys 
• the women’s soccer team 
• the women’ soccer team 
• the men’s clothes 
• the men’ clothes 
Confusing Case #3 
What do you do if the original word or name already ends in S? 
There’s actually a debate about this among native English speakers, and people do it 
different ways. 
Most people put ‘s, but there is a minority of people who put only the apostrophe: 
• Chris’s computer / Chris’ computer 
• my boss’s desk / my boss’ desk 
• Jesus’s words / Jesus’ words 
• Kansas’s population / Kansas’ population 
I personally put the ‘s after Chris and boss because we usually pronounce it like this: 
• Chrisis computer 
• my bossis desk 
But in other words, especially those that already have lots of S’s (Jesus, Kansas, 
Massachusetts), I would put only the apostrophe because we probably would not 
pronounce the extra ‘s. 
Confusing Case #4 
Can you have two possessives in a row? 
Yes! You can say, for example: 
• Sarah’s mother’s friend 
• My cousin’s husband’s ex-wife 
This structure is not especially common, but it is possible. You can also say: 


• A friend of Sarah’s mother 
• The ex-wife of my cousin’s husband 
 
Confusing Case #5 
What about when you have two people? 
Well, then we need to ask the question – do they share possession, or do they each 
have their own? 
If they share possession, then we put ‘s only after the second name: 
• Shared possession: Bill and Jen's house 
For example, if Bill and Jen are married and living together in one house. 
But if each person has their own thing, then we put ‘s after each name, and make the 
thing plural: 
• Separate possession: Doug’s and Steve’s houses 
In this sentence, Doug has his own house, and Steve has his own separate house. We 
are talking about two houses. 
 
Possessive word vs. “of” 
What about situations like “my country’s flag” or “the flag of my country”?  
Unfortunately there are no definite rules that cover every situation – but here are 
some guidelines. 
The first thing to ask is if the "possessor" is living or not living. 
If the possessor is living (a person or animal) then we usually use 's: 
• My sister's car broke down. 
The car of my sister broke down. 
• The party is at the Johnsons' house. 
• My dog's name is Muffin. 
• Peter's reputation is stellar. 


There is an exception if the living thing is a long phrase and not just one or two 
words. In these cases, we would probably rephrase the sentence using different 
words instead of a possessive: 
• That girl with blonde hair’s necklace is pretty. 
• That girl with blonde hair has / is wearing a pretty necklace. 
• The guy over there’s car won’t start. 
• The car belonging to that guy over there won’t start. 
We also typically use ‘s with time and money, even though these are not living 
things: 
• three hundred dollars' worth of jewelry 
• two years' experience 
• Monday's meeting 
• yesterday's discussion 
• next week's festival 
If the possessor is not living, then we usually use "of," although the 's form can be 
used for some nouns: 
• the roof of the house 
• the price of this product 
• the edge of the table 
• the cover of the book 
the book's cover 
• the border of the country 
the country's border 
• the economy of Australia 
Australia’s economy 
the Australian economy 
We often try to avoid too many "of the"s in a row, so for example, instead of saying: 
• the repair of the plumbing of the building 
• the repair of the building's plumbing 
Also, if the possessor is multiple words, then we prefer to use of: 
• the cover of the latest book in the series 
• the latest book in the series’ cover 


When relating a part of an object to the whole, when the part has no meaning except 
as it relates to the whole, we always use of: 
• the top of the stairs 
the stairs' top 
• the side of the house  
the house's side 
• the middle of the room 
the room's middle 
• part of the problem 
the problem's part 
Now, when the part is a distinct object itself, then we can often say it either way, or 
even using compound noun: 
• the windows of the hotel 
the hotel's windows 
• the crust of the pie 
the pie's crust / the pie crust 
• the antenna of the radio 
the radio's antenna / the radio antenna 
We also use of when talking about units in a group: 
• a collection of paintings 
a paintings’ collection 
• the group of buildings 
• a bunch of markers 
• half of my family 
What about titles, like the President of the United States or the Queen of England? 
Many of them use of, although some may have alternatives: 
• the President of the United States 
• the Queen of England 
• the Prime Minister of India  
the Indian Prime Minister 
India's Prime Minister 
• the Director of Marketing 
the Marketing Director 


Again, there are no concrete rules here – it often goes by the way the expression is 
typically said by most people, or the way the speaker thinks sounds better.

Summary 
• Some possessive pronouns have different forms depending on whether they 
are used before a noun, or after it / by themselves. Ex) That's my book / That 
book is mine 
• It's is short for "it is," and its signifies possession 
• If a noun is two words, add 's 
• If a noun is plural and ends in S, add only an apostrophe 
• If a singular noun ends in S, we sometimes add 's and sometimes only ' 
• For two people sharing possession, put the 's after the second name. Ex) Bill 
and Jen's house 
• For two people with separate possessions, put an 's after both names. Ex) 
Doug's and Steve's houses 
• With people, animals, time, and money, we typically use 's and not of. 
• With inanimate objects, we usually use of (although there are exceptions). 
• With people’s titles, we often use of (although there are sometimes 
alternatives).
