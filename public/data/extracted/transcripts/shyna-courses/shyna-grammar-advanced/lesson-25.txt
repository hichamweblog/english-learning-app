Lesson 25: Position of Adjectives 
Adjectives are words that describe nouns. We can use adjectives before the noun, or 
following a linking verb such as appear, be, become, get, feel, look, seem: 
• Her eyes are blue. 
• She has blue eyes. 
• This bread looks fresh. 
• Fresh bread is delicious. 
Some adjectives are never used before the noun; they are only used after the linking 
verb: 
• The boy was alone in the room. 
The alone boy 
• The baby is asleep. 
The asleep baby 
• The leaders seem to be aware of the problem. 
The aware leaders 
These include a number of "a" words like afraid, alike, alive, alone, ashamed, 
asleep, awake, and aware. Usually these adjectives have another version that we 
can put before the noun: 
• The child was afraid. 
The frightened child ran to his mother. 
• The boy was alone. 
A lone boy sat in the classroom. 
• These two books seem very much alike. 
I've read two similar books lately. 
• The baby is asleep. 
The sleeping baby is wrapped in a blanket. 
One exception to the “adjectives before nouns” rule is that adjectives come after 
words like something, anybody, nothing: 
• I want to go someplace warm on vacation. 
• There's nothing interesting in the news. 
 


And a few nouns ending in -able/-ible can also be used after nouns: 
• We're prepared for every situation imaginable. 
We're prepared for every imaginable situation. 
• He took the last seat available. 
He took the last available seat.

What do you notice about the first adjective in each series? Is it an opinion 
adjective or a fact adjective? 
It’s an opinion adjective. The first rule to remember is that opinion adjectives 
usually come before fact adjectives. 
Some other examples of opinion adjectives include: 
• good / bad / great / terrible 
• beautiful / pretty / sexy / comfortable 
• ugly / awful / strange / uncomfortable 
• delicious / disgusting / tasty / nasty 
• important / excellent / wonderful / brilliant 
• funny / interesting / boring

Purpose 
 
 
“Purpose” adjectives are almost like part of the noun itself. They describe its 
function or what it is used for: 
• running shoes 
• a sleeping bag 
• a flower vase 
• a frying pan 
• a tennis racket 
It’s rare to use more than 3 adjectives. But the adjectives you do use should follow 
this order approximately. There may be some exceptions! 


With time adjectives, we put first/next/last or past before the number: 
• the first two weeks 
the two first weeks 
• the past ten minutes 
the ten past minutes 
 
Whether or not to use and 
When there are two or more color adjectives, use and: 
• yellow and orange flowers 
• a red, white, and blue flag 
We usually don't use and with other adjectives if they are before a noun: 
• a round wooden table 
a round and wooden table 
• long brown hair 
long and brown hair 
However, we can use and with other adjectives if they are after the noun: 
• The day was hot and humid. 
• The clouds were big, white, and fluffy. 
There are other adjectives that don’t fall into the categories above. For these, the 
essential rule to remember is that opinion adjectives always come before fact 
adjectives (appearance and other descriptive adjectives): 
• delicious (opinion) organic (fact) food 
• crazy (opinion) religious (fact) people 
• interesting (opinion) cultural (fact) traditions 
• confusing (opinion) financial (fact) data 
Although studying the order of adjectives in English can help… there reaches a point 
where you’ll learn them best simply by seeing and hearing them in action.  
Most native English speakers don’t know the rules of adjective order at all – we just 
know that it “sounds right” to say “the big red ball” and “sounds wrong” to say “the 
red big ball.” 


Compound Adjectives 
Some adjectives have more than one word. For example: 
• a well-known author 
• an English-speaking country 
• a three-hour movie 
• a part-time job 
• a middle-aged woman 
These are called compound adjectives. When they are before a noun, we use a 
hyphen – but when they are after a noun and the verb “to be” or another linking 
verb, we do not: 
• This is a world-famous museum. 
This museum is world famous. 
• It was quite a thought-provoking book. 
The book was quite thought provoking. 
The exception is with adverbs ending in –LY. These do not require a hyphen: 
• He is a widely recognized expert. 
• His work is widely recognized.  
There is an additional detail with compound adjectives involving numbers – when 
they are before the noun, we do not include –s: 
• I have a ten-year-old daughter. 
My daughter is ten years old. 
• Let’s take a five-minute break. 
Let’s take a break for five minutes.

Summary 
• Adjectives describe nouns. They can be placed immediately before the noun 
(blue eyes) or after the verb "to be" or another linking verb (Her eyes are 
blue). 
• Some adjectives, such as afraid and alone, cannot be placed immediately 
before the noun - they must be after a linking verb. 
• A few words ending in -able and -ible can be placed either before or after the 
noun, for example: the last seat available / the last available seat 
• When there are multiple adjectives, opinion adjectives come before fact 
adjectives. 


• The fact adjectives usually follow the order of Size, Shape, Age, Color, 
Nationality/Origin, Material, Purpose 
• When adjectives come before the noun, we only use the word and between 
two color adjectives: 
o a red and yellow jacket 
o the old, run-down house 
• When they come after the noun, then we can use and: 
o the house was old and run-down 
• Compound adjectives are made of two words, and they often contain the 
past or present participle: well-known, long-lasting 
• If they are before the noun, we use a hyphen; if they are after the noun and 
a linking verb, we do not.
