Lesson 14: 
Verbs + TO or –ING with a Change in Meaning 
This is the final part of our series on verb-verb combinations. In Lesson 12, you 
learned that some verbs can be followed by either TO or –ING with no difference in 
meaning. For example: 
• 
I like to swim. = I like swimming. 
However, there are a handful of verbs for which 
the meaning or use changes depending on 
whether the second verb is in the TO form or 
the –ING form – and that’s what we’re going to 
study today. 
First, you're going to hear a dialogue between Dora (who is British) and Cathy (who 
is American) that shows seven of these verbs in action. Pay attention to how each 
verb is used – then read the explanation of the differences. 
Dialogue 
Dora: Hi Cathy! How was your weekend? 
Well, it was... interesting, to say the least. 
Dora: Do tell! 
Cathy: I convinced Jim to take Friday afternoon off 
so we could take a little weekend getaway to our 
beach house – remember it? You've been there. 
Dora: Right, that's the place where you had a big 
New Year's bash a few years ago. I'll 
never forget Mary dancing on top of your brand-
new living room table - until it collapsed! 


Cathy: Don't remind me! Whatever happened to Mary, by the way? 
I remember her saying she was looking for work, but I haven't heard from her in a 
while. I’ve been meaning to give her a call, but I’ve been so busy. 
Dora: Oh, she finished her degree and went on to get a job at the New York Times. 
Cathy: No kidding! Good for her. So anyway – we left after lunch 
on Friday and got there around 5, only to find that Jim 
had forgotten to bring the key. 
Dora: Ouch! How did that happen? 
Cathy: It’s usually on his keychain, but he had taken it off to make an extra copy for 
our son, and just didn’t remember to put it back on. 
Dora: So what did you do? 
Cathy: Jim tried to pick the lock for over an hour – I kept telling him it wouldn’t 
work, but he just grunted and went on trying. 
Dora: He probably didn’t want to admit defeat – since that would mean driving 5 
hours back to your house to get the key! 
Cathy: Not necessarily – he then changed tactics and tried prying open one of the 
windows. It took some effort, but he managed to do it. 
Dora: Aha – so you got in. 
Cathy: Yes, but that’s not the end of the story. 
Twenty minutes later there was a knock at the 
door – it was the police. 
Dora: The police?! 
Cathy: Yup. It turns out our new next-door 
neighbor, who we hadn’t met yet, saw Jim 
breaking in through the window and thought he 
was a robber. Jim tried to explain that we were 
the owners of the house, but of course we didn’t 


have the documents handy, so the police said, “We regret to inform you that we’ll 
need to take you into custody until this can be sorted out.” What could we do? 
We stopped arguing with them and went to the police station. 
Dora: And then what happened? 
Cathy: We called our son and asked him to bring the title to the house. He set off 
right away, but his car started having mechanical problems and he had to stop to 
get it fixed – and the mechanic said it would only be ready on Saturday at noon. 
Dora: What a nightmare! So you spent the night at the police station? 
Cathy: Yes – and it gets worse. 
Dora: Really? 
Cathy: After our son arrived and we were able to prove 
ourselves to be the rightful owners of the house, we 
drove back there – only to find that we had left that 
window open, and… 
Dora: …don’t tell me. You’d been robbed. 
Cathy: Bingo. They took everything that wasn’t nailed 
down – so we spent the rest of the day at the police 
station, giving a report of all the stolen items. Needless 
to say, I really regret taking that trip! 
 
 
 
 
 
 


Changes in Meaning 
Verb #1 – Forget 
Use forget + to + verb for a task you are supposed to do: 
• 
Jim had forgotten to bring the key. 
• 
We forgot to close the window of the beach house. 
• 
Don’t forget to go to the bank tomorrow. 
Use forget + -ING for an experience in the past. This is usually used in the form “I’ll 
never forget ________ing” – for a very memorable experience. 
• 
I’ll never forget Mary dancing on top of the table. 
• 
We’ll never forget spending three weeks in Tahiti on our honeymoon. 
• 
My son will never forget winning first place in the karate tournament – it 
was a huge accomplishment. 
Put it into practice! 
What’s one thing you’ve recently forgotten to do? 
• I forgot to… 
What’s one experience you’ll never forget? 
• I’ll never forget _________ing… 
Verb #2 – Remember 
This is very similar to forget. 
Use remember + to + verb for a task: 
• 
Please remember to turn off the lights when you leave. 
• 
Jim didn’t remember to put the key back on his keychain. 
• 
Thankfully I remembered to bring my umbrella – it rained all weekend. 


Use remember + -ing for an experience in the past: 
• 
I remember Mary saying she was looking for work. 
• 
Where are my sunglasses? I remember 
putting them on this table. 
• 
He doesn’t remember receiving your letter – could 
you send it again? 
Put it into practice! 
What’s one task from your to-do list that you need to remember to do? 
• I need to remember to… 
What’s one thing from your childhood that you remember doing? 
• I remember _______ing… 
Verb #3 – Go on 
When we say someone went on to do something, it’s in the context of something 
they did later in life (usually something positive): 
• 
Mary finished her degree and went on to get a job at the New York Times. 
• 
After becoming famous as a singer, he went on to start an acting career. 
• 
My daughter was lazy as a teenager, but then she went on to become a very 
responsible adult. 
Went on + -ing means “continued.” 
• 
Jim went on attempting to pick the lock even 
though I told him it wouldn’t work. 
• 
I told him I needed to get off the phone, but he 
just went on talking for another 20 minutes. 
• 
You shouldn’t go on thinking about your mistakes – 
just forget them and move on with your life. 
Put it into practice! 


What did one of your old friends go on to do later in life? 
• He/she went on to… 
Verb #4 – Mean 
Mean + to + verb is used for intentions: 
• 
I’ve been meaning (intending) to call Mary, but I’ve been too busy. 
• 
Oops – I meant to say the company was founded in 1894, not 1984. 
• 
They’re saving up money – I think they mean to buy a house next year. 
Mean + -ing is used when something will result in something else being necessary: 
• 
If we can’t pick the lock, that means driving 5 
hours back to get the key. 
(failure to pick the lock will result in the need to 
drive 5 hours back) 
• 
Taking that job means moving to another country. 
(if you take that job, the result is that you need to 
move to another country) 
• 
When I became a vegetarian, it meant giving 
up hamburgers and hot dogs. 
Put it into practice! 
What’s one thing you’ve been meaning to do? (intending to do) 
• I’ve been meaning to… 
Verb #5 – Regret 
Regret + to + verb is typically used with “inform” or (less commonly) “announce” – 
it is a polite or formal way to give unpleasant news: 
• 
We regret to inform you that the deadline has already passed for 
applications to this position. 
• 
We regret to announce that this year’s festival has been canceled. 


Regret + -ing form is used when you regret a past action; you wish you had not 
done it: 
• 
Cathy regrets taking the trip to the beach house. 
• 
I regret dropping out of college – I wish I had finished 
my degree. 
• 
We regret moving to this city; it’s a rather boring 
place. 
Put it into practice! 
What’s one thing you regret doing? 
• I regret _________ing… 
Verb #6 – Stop 
This time I’m going to switch the order and talk about stop + -ing first! 
Use stop + ing to talk about an activity you were doing, but 
then stopped: 
• 
Cathy and Jim stopped arguing with the police. 
• 
Stop looking at your cell phone when I’m trying to talk 
to you! 
• 
I stopped smoking two years ago. 
Use stop + to + verb when you stop a different activity (which is not mentioned) to 
DO the second verb. For example: 
• 
Jim and Cathy’s son stopped to get his car fixed. 
(technically, he stopped driving in order to get his car fixed – although we 
don’t mention driving in the sentence; it is understood from the context) 
• 
My exercise class is really intense; I always have to stop to drink some water. 
(you stop exercising in order to drink water) 
• 
Bob was working on the computer, but he stopped to help his daughter with 
her homework. 
(he stopped working in order to do the second action: to help) 


Maybe seeing it visually will help: 
 
Put it into practice! 
What’s one bad habit you need to stop doing? 
• I need to stop _________ing… 
 
Verb #7 – Try 
Try is one of the most interesting verbs. When you say someone tried to 
do something, it implies that they weren’t successful: 
• 
Jim tried to pick the lock of the beach house, but he couldn’t do it. 
• 
I tried to read the novel, but I found it boring. 
• 
We tried to call you, but your cell phone was turned off. 
When you say tried + -ing, it simply means they attempted – and that attempt could 
be successful or not: 


• 
Jim tried prying open the window – and succeeded! 
• 
I couldn’t figure out what was wrong with my computer, but I tried 
restarting it, and that seems to have fixed the problem. 
• 
I tried reading the novel, but I found it boring. 
• 
We tried calling you, but your cell phone was turned off. 
This difference only applies when using tried (the verb in the past). When you 
use try in the present or future, then try to and try + ing are more or less the 
same… although “try to” has more of the sense of “make an effort to accomplish a 
goal, even though it’s difficult” and “try + -ing” has more of the sense of “doing an 
experiment, to see if it will solve a problem.” 
• 
I’m trying to lose weight. 
= making an effort to accomplish a difficult goal 
• 
“My stuff won’t fit in my suitcase.” 
“Try rolling up your clothes instead of folding them. Or if that doesn’t 
work, try putting them inside plastic bags and squeezing the air out.” 
These things aren’t difficult, they’re just suggested solutions to a problem 
Put it into practice! 
Think about one thing you tried to do in the past and failed: 
• I tried to __________, but ____________ 
                                               (result) 
Think about one thing you tried doing to solve a problem in the past, and succeeded: 
• I tried _______ing, and ____________ 
     (result)
