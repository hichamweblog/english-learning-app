Lesson 38: Other ways of adding 
information to sentences 
In yesterday’s lesson, you learned how to add information with relative clauses. This 
helps link your sentences and make them richer in detail.  
Today’s topic continues this theme – we’ll cover four more ways to add information 
to sentences, and learn a major mistake that even native English speakers 
sometimes make when writing. 
1. Prepositional phrases 
This is a simple way to add information to a sentence, and we often do it naturally in 
our sentences: 
• That house is for sale. It is on the street corner. 
 The house on the street corner is for sale. 
• He has a car. The car has a powerful sound system. 
 He has a car with a powerful sound system. 
• The frying pan is in the drawer. The drawer is under the sink. 
 The frying pan is in the drawer under the sink. 
Beware of separating the prepositional phrase from the noun it describes. This can 
cause the sentence to be confusing or make no sense. For example: 
• A woman was talking with black hair. 
What? “Black hair” can’t talk with anybody! 
• A woman with black hair was talking. 
The phrase “with black hair” describes the woman. 
• The birds are from the rainforest in the cage. 
A rainforest can’t be in a cage. 
• The birds in the cage are from the rainforest. 
The phrase “in the cage” describes the birds. 
2. Additional noun phrases 
When we want to describe a person, place, or thing in a different way, we can add a 
noun phrase enclosed in commas. For example: 


• Dr. Johnson, an expert in child psychology, will give the seminar. 
• Juicing, the newest diet craze, is popular among women. 
• The company is based in Austin, a city with an exciting startup scene. 
Each of these sentences could be rewritten with who is and which is inside the 
clause: 
• Dr. Johnson, who is an expert in child psychology, will give the seminar. 
• Juicing, which is the newest diet craze, is popular among women. 
• The company is based in Austin, which is a city with an exciting startup 
scene. 
We can omit who is and which is only when the verb in the clause is “is” or another 
form of the verb “to be.” For example, in the following sentences we must include 
who and which because the main verb is not a form of “is”: 
• Dr. Johnson, who wrote a book on child psychology, will give the seminar. 
• Juicing, which recently became popular, is mostly practiced by women. 
• The company is based in Austin, which has a population of 900,000. 
3. Participle clauses  
A third way to add extra information to a sentence is by using a participle clause 
starting with –ING or –ED. For example: 
• I really like the music playing in the background of the video. 
• The people working on the project are quite dedicated.  
• The tools needed for the repair were very specialized. 
• Each student selected for a scholarship will receive $10,000. 
Of course, we can also rewrite these sentences: 
• the music that is playing in the background 
• the people who are working on the project 
• the tools that were needed for this repair 
• each student who was selected for a scholarship 
But again, when the verb in the relative clause is a form of “to be,” we can leave 
these two words out and simply start the clause with the –ING or –ED word. 
In English, not all past participles end in –ED, so there are some irregular ones: 


• I always loved the stories told by my grandmother. 
• The tomatoes grown in that region are delicious. 
• A country run by corrupt leaders will never prosper. 
And we can also have the structure being + past participle – usually for things 
happening in the future or right now, immediately, in this moment: 
• The article being published tomorrow will make a big impact. 
(future) 
• The show being filmed in Studio 3 is a documentary. 
(happening at this moment) 
4. Participle clauses with adverbial meaning 
You might be wondering what that title means! Again, don’t worry too much about 
the technical terms – when you see the examples, it will become clear. 
These are simply clauses that start with a present participle (-ING form) or past 
participle (-ED or irregular) that tell us about timing, causes/results, and conditions. 
For example: 
• Stepping onto the stage, Laura was welcomed with applause. 
(time – WHEN Laura stepped onto the stage…) 
• Hoping to get an interview, Ryan sent his resume to the company. 
(reason – Ryan sent his resume BECAUSE he was hoping to get an interview) 
• Performed properly, this strategy is very effective. 
(condition – IF the strategy is performed properly, THEN it is very effective) 
We will study these in more depth in tomorrow’s lesson. However, there is one 
major mistake to avoid – it is called a dangling participle. 
You’ll notice that in each of these examples, the participle phrase is right next to the 
person or thing it describes. Laura is the one who stepped onto the stage; Ryan is 
the one who was hoping to get an interview, and this strategy is what must be 
performed properly. 
The mistake is if we structure the sentence like this: 
• Stepping onto the stage, the audience welcomed Laura with applause. 
(this makes it sound like “the audience” stepped onto the stage, not Laura) 


• Hoping to get an interview, Ryan’s resume was very well written. 
(this makes it sound like the resume itself wanted to be interviewed) 
Therefore, the participle clause must be next to the person/thing that it describes. 
This mistake can even lead to some really silly sentences, for example: 
• Walking through the park, the trees were beautiful. 
(this implies that the trees were actually walking!) 
• Walking through the park, we admired the beautiful trees. 
(this sentence is OK – we are the ones walking through the park) 
This was just a quick introduction to participle phrases, and we’ll learn more about 
them in tomorrow’s lesson. 
 
Summary 
Four additional ways to add information to sentences: 
1. Prepositional phrases 
The house on the street corner is for sale. 
2. Additional noun phrases 
Dr. Johnson, an expert in child psychology, will give the seminar. 
3. Participle clauses 
I really like the music playing in the background of the video. 
The tools needed for this repair were very specialized. 
The tomatoes grown in that region are delicious. 
4. Participle clauses with adverbial meaning 
These tell us about timing, causes/results, and conditions 
Stepping onto the stage, Laura was welcomed with applause. 
Performed properly, this strategy is very effective. 
These clauses should always go immediately next to the person/thing they describe, 
in order to avoid misunderstandings and ridiculous sentences: 


• Walking through the park, the trees were beautiful. 
• Walking through the park, we admired the beautiful trees.
