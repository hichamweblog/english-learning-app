Lesson 23: There & It 
Use there when we talk about something for the first time, to say that it exists: 
• There's a new café on Main Street. 
• There are several books in my backpack. 
• There were eight applicants for the job. 
• There has been a change in plans. 
• There will be a lot of people at the concert. 
As you can see, you can use there with different forms of the verb to be to talk 
about things in the past, present, and future. 
Use it to refer to something we have mentioned previously. 
• We went to the new café. It’s really nice. 
• How was the traffic?  
It was terrible. 
• I read the book and I really liked it. 
Compare these examples to see the difference between there and it: 
• There’s a package waiting for you at home. 
• There’s a package waiting for you at home. It was delivered this morning. 
• There was a typo in the document. 
• I found a typo in the document and fixed it. 
Simple, right? Let’s look at each one in more detail. 
There 
Negative forms 
To talk about the absence of something, you can say it one of two ways: 
• There’s no milk in the fridge. 
• There isn’t any milk in the fridge. 
• There was nobody in the office. 
• There wasn’t anybody in the office. 
• There are no batteries in the toy. 
• There aren’t any batteries in the toy. 


However, be sure to avoid mixing them and creating a double negative! 
• There isn’t no milk in the fridge. 
• There wasn’t nobody in the office. 
 
Singular and plural 
We can abbreviate there is to there’s. However, we cannot abbreviate there are to 
there’re (even though it often sounds like this in spoken English): 
• There’s a bird in the tree. 
• There are two birds in the tree. 
Sounds like: Therere two birds in the tree. 
When you have a series of two, three, or more single objects, we use there is (not 
there are): 
• There is an apple and a banana in the bowl. 
• There's a pencil, an eraser, and some paper on the desk. 
If one of the objects in the list is plural, then we say there is or there are based oon 
the noun closest to it: 
• There’s a dog and two cats in the yard. 
• There are two cats and a dog in the yard. 
To use there with a lot of or lots of, it depends on whether the following noun is 
uncountable or plural/countable: 
• There’s a lot of frosting on the cake. 
• There are a lot of cookies in the box. 
• There’s lots of information in this book. 
• There are lots of articles about this topic. 
In informal English, some people simply use there’s all the time, even with plural 
nouns. This is not correct, but it is common: 
• There’s a lot of fun things to do in this town. 
• There’s about twenty children in the class.

There with other verbs 
You can also use there with modal verbs in the present: 
• There must be something we can do to help. 
• Don’t put your hand into that hole. There might/could be a snake in there. 
• There should be some books about Roman history in the library. 
And with modal verbs in the past: 
• There must have been tons of fans waiting for the celebrity when he arrived. 
• The road is closed. There might have been an accident. 
• There should have been a team of five people helping the customers; how 
come only two of you were working? 
As well as with a few state verbs: 
• There appears/seems to be a problem with the internet. 
• There tend to be more tourists in the area in January. 
 
It 
As mentioned earlier in the lesson, we use it for something we have already 
mentioned – but let me caution you about two common mistakes. One is forgetting 
to use it when the sentence needs a subject, or when the verb needs an object: 
• I bought a new jacket. Is black. 
• My microwave broke, but my husband fixed. 


The other common mistake is repeating "it" immediately after the subject was first 
mentioned – this is unnecessary: 
• The food it’s is not very good. 
• My favorite book it’s is War and Peace. 
So here are four situations where we use it in English: 
1. As the subject of a sentence when we have mentioned the 
thing recently. 
This can be in two separate sentences, or in the same sentence when beginning a 
new clause: 
• Where’s my wallet? It’s not in my purse. 
• I bought a shirt for my son, but it was too small. 
2. As the direct object with verbs that need a direct object, and 
we have mentioned the thing recently. 
Again, this can be in two separate sentences or in the same one: 
• Is the window still open? 
No, I closed it. 
• I caught the ball, but then I dropped it. 
3. In expressions with distance, time, and weather: 
• How far is it to Boston? 
It's about five miles. 
• How long does it take to change a tire? 
It takes 10 minutes. 
• It's a nice day. 
• It's raining. 
• It was sunny. 
• It will be cloudy. 
4. Use it as the subject when the subject is a long clause 
• It's not fair that she's getting paid more than me for the same work. 


• It was surprising that he was able to accomplish his goal. 
= The fact that he was able to accomplish his goal was surprising. 
• It hasn’t been proven whether or not he committed the crime. 
This structure is often used with emotions: 
• It annoys me when people use up the toilet paper and don’t replace the roll. 
• It’s shocking that such blatant lies are taught in school. 
• It was inspiring to see how many people donated to the animal shelter. 
We also have the structures I like/love/enjoy/hate (it) when… - in these 
sentences, it is optional, but most native speakers include it: 
 
• I love it when my kids offer to wash the dishes. 
= I love when my kids offer to wash the dishes. 
• I hate it when people don’t return my calls. 
• I can’t stand it when you take my stuff without asking! 
We also tend to use it with some verbs of judgment: 
• It’s good that you’re studying English every day. 
• It’s a shame that you missed the party. 
• It’s wonderful when someone believes in your potential. 
• It’s terrible when people turn a blind eye to child abuse. 
And some verbs of perception and interpretation: 
• It seems like you’re upset. Is everything OK? 
• Ouch. It feels like I pulled a muscle in my back. 
• I find it difficult to understand Australian English. 
= I find understanding Australian English difficult. 
• It hit me / It dawned on me that he’d been right all along. 
These expressions mean “I realized” or “I suddenly understood” 
• I think/believe/consider it an advantage that our company is small.  
• I see/regard it as a lack of respect when people are late for appointments.

Summary 
• Use there to talk about something for the first time. Use it to refer back to 
something we have already mentioned. 
• There can be used with various forms of the word to be, as well as modal 
verbs and some state verbs like seems or appears. 
• Say there's with singular nouns and uncountable nouns. Say there are with 
plural nouns. 
• When you have items in a list, the word there matches the first noun closest 
to it. 
• Use it in four situations: 
o As the subject of a sentence or clause, when we have mentioned the 
thing recently. Ex) I bought a new jacket. It’s black. 
o As the direct object with verbs that need one, when we have mentioned 
the thing recently. Ex) I caught the ball, but then I dropped it. 
o In expressions with distance, time, and weather 
o When the subject is a long clause
