Lesson 22 - Compound Nouns 
A compound noun is a noun that is composed of two or more words. 
Compound nouns can be: 
• One word – toothbrush, cupcake 
• Two words – flower pot, high school 
• A hyphenated word – self-esteem, merry-go-round 
There is no rule that defines which of these forms a compound noun takes, so if in 
doubt, check a dictionary. 
Compound nouns can be formed in a couple of different ways: 
Noun + Noun

Pronunciation of Compound Nouns 


When pronouncing compound nouns, the stress is usually on the first word: 
• HEADphones 
• PAPER clip 
• DOWNtime 
• GETaway 
• SWIMMING pool 
When saying the compound noun GREENhouse (a special transparent structure to 
grow plants) – the emphasis is on the word green. When saying GREEN HOUSE, 
there is approximately equal emphasis on both words, or a slightly stronger 
emphasis on “house.” 
There are occasional exceptions – in the word great-grandfather, the emphasis is on 
grandfather. But in general, compound nouns have the stress on the first word.

Singular & Plural of Compound Nouns 
Notice that the first noun usually has the singular form. We say apple tree and not 
apples tree, even though the tree has many apples. We say toothbrush and not 
teethbrush, even though the brush is used on many teeth. 
There are a few exceptions: 
• a savings account 
(where you keep your money at the bank) 
• a customs officer 
(who checks to see if anyone is bringing illegal items into the country) 
• the arms trade 
(“arms” = weapons, “trade” = buying and selling) 
• a glasses case 
(a protective case for your glasses) 
• a clothesline 
(where you can hang your clothes to dry) 
• an arts festival 
(involves multiple arts) 
• a publications department 
(part of a company that produces publications) 
• a news broadcast 
(the word “news” always has an “s”) 
Another exception is when the first noun is a user of the second noun – in this case, 
we often use 's: 
• a bird's nest, a lion's den, a girls' school, the men's room 
When talking about parts of the body, we use 's for the possessive form:  
• the lady's hair 
• the boy's arms 
But when talking about parts of objects, we usually use a noun + noun compound 
word, or "of": 
• a table leg 
a table’s leg 
• the TV screen 


• a shirt collar 
• a branch of the company 
• the lid of the jar 
Notice this difference when talking about cups and glasses: 
• a tea cup refers to the empty cup itself 
• a cup of tea is full of tea 
• a wine glass refers to the empty glass itself 
• a glass of wine is full of wine 
Now, what if we want to make a compound noun plural? 
We usually just add –s to the final word: 
• toothbrushes 
• paper clips 
• apple trees 
• swimming pools 
• vice-presidents 
If it’s a structure using “of,” add –s to the main word: 
• two cups of tea 
• two cup of teas 
• three Ministers of Defense 
• three Minister of Defenses

Nouns Formed from Phrasal Verbs 
Some nouns are formed from phrasal verbs. The phrasal verb is two words, and the 
noun is one word, for example: 
• Can you hand out these papers? 
• I need to print some handouts for class. 
• I mixed up the documents. 
• Sorry about the mix-up. 
• The airline is giving away a free round-trip ticket. 
• Let’s enter the giveaway to see if we’ll win! 
This can’t be done for every phrasal verb. For example, another word for “hand out” 
is “give out,” yet there is no such noun as “giveout.”  
Here are 10 examples of compound nouns formed from phrasal verbs. See if you can 
guess their meaning from the context: 
1) Tryouts for the local baseball team will be held one month before the season 
starts. 
2) The movie was great, but the ending was a bit of a letdown. 
3) Public health officials are working to combat a sudden outbreak of disease. 
4) The CEO’s poor decisions led to the downfall of the company. 
5) I really like the layout of this website; it’s easy to find things. 
6) This seaside hotel is the perfect place to spend a weekend getaway. 
7) After Christmas dinner, we had a lot of leftovers in the fridge. 
8) There have been problems with this project ever since the outset. 
9) I had a religious upbringing. 
10) 
Both sides blamed each other for the breakdown of negotiations.

Writing Task 
As society changes, the language changes as well. Today many of the compound 
nouns for people’s professions, which used to end with “man,” are being modified – 
for example, we have fireman, firewoman, and the gender-neutral firefighter.  
In your country, which jobs are considered more as “men’s jobs” and which jobs are 
considered “women’s jobs”? Is this changing at all? What difficulties do you imagine 
someone faces when they have a profession that is considered “typical” of the 
opposite gender? Send me your text at shayna@espressoenglish.net for feedback 
and correction!
