Lesson 18: Countable & Uncountable Nouns 
Countable nouns are things that can be counted. Most nouns in English are 
countable: 
• I have a dog. My friend has two dogs. 
• We bought a chair. They bought four chairs. 
• He has an idea. She has many ideas. 
Uncountable nouns are things we typically view as a single entity or as a “mass” 
(some teachers call these “mass nouns”). We cannot "count" them or divide them 
into separate elements. 
• She gave me a lot of advice. 
• There's some sand in my shoes. 
• He doesn't have any money. 
• I put a little milk in my coffee. 
Uncountable nouns tend to fall into the following categories: 
Liquids and gases 
water, coffee, milk, air 
Materials 
metal, wood, rubber, plastic 
Substances made of many 
tiny bits 
sand, rice 
Words involving energy 
electricity, heat, radiation 
Categories or groups 
fruit, food, furniture, music 
Abstract concepts 
happiness, information, 
intelligence, safety 
 
However, keep in mind that countable and uncountable is NOT the same as physical 
and non-physical objects.

Rules with Uncountable Nouns 
1. They cannot be plural. 
You cannot say: 
• informations, furnitures, advices, happinesses, musics, equipments 
There are some exceptions. Sometimes when ordering drinks, we say “I’ll have a 
small coffee” or “Can we have three beers?” This is simply a short way of saying “a 
small cup of coffee” or “three bottles/cans of beer.” 
Another exception is when we are talking about several different types of an 
uncountable noun – we often make the uncountable noun plural: 
• Many different metals are used in construction. 
metals = types of metal 
• I love tropical fruits. 
fruits = types of fruit 
• These oils can be used to soften your skin. 
oils = types of oil 
• All the wines in this store are very expensive. 
wines = bottles of wine or types of wine 
Other nouns that can be used this way include cheese, food, bread, and meat. 
2. You can't use a/an with them (but you can use "some" and "any") 
I need an information. 
I need some information. 
He didn't have a luggage. 
He didn't have any luggage. 
Some of the concepts of countable/uncountable might be different from your native 
language. For example, in your native language it might be possible to say 
"informations" - but in English, it is not. 
The idea of countable and uncountable might seem like a useless thing to learn - but 
it’s very important because it affects many of the words that you can use together 
with these nouns. 
If you say, "I have a lot of things," that is a perfectly good sentence. 


If you say, "I have a lot of stuffs," it sounds funny. Stuff is uncountable, so it cannot 
be plural. 
If you say, "I ate too much rice," that is a correct sentence. 
If you say, "I ate too many rice" or "I ate too much cookies," those are both 
incorrect. Many is only used with countable nouns, and much is only used with 
uncountable nouns. We would need to switch them: 
• I ate too much rice. 
• I ate too many cookies.

Quantifying Uncountable Nouns 
Sometimes we do want to quantify how much of an uncountable noun we are talking 
about. We can do this using another “measuring” word: 
• a slice of bread 
• two cups of sugar 
• three bags of rice 
• ten bottles of wine 
• many packages of pasta 
With substances like rice and sand, the individual bits are called grains. 


And with nouns like information, advice, equipment, and furniture, we can use the 
word "piece”: 
• a new piece of equipment 
• three important pieces of advice 
• many pieces of antique furniture 
• a key piece of information

Nouns that are both Countable AND Uncountable  
Finally, we have some nouns that can be either countable or uncountable depending 
on the situation. I’ll teach you seven of them: 
1. chicken 
countable – the animal 
We have fifty chickens on our farm. 
uncountable – the food 
Would you like some chicken? 
2. paper 
countable – individual documents 
I showed the immigration agent my papers. 
uncountable – paper in general 
Don’t put too much paper in the printer, it might jam. 
(for an individual paper, we can say a sheet of paper / piece of paper) 
3. time 
countable – specific moments 
I’ve been to Japan three times. 
uncountable – the general concept of time 
There was too little time to finish the task. 
4. experience 
countable – specific experiences 
Studying in London was a great experience. 
uncountable – experience in general 
He has great experience in his profession. 
5. room 
countable – the spaces in a house 
My apartment has two rooms. 
uncountable – space in general 
I’ll make some room for these books in the bookshelf. 
6. light 
countable – light bulbs 
The Christmas tree was covered with many lights. 
uncountable – light in general 
There’s not much light in here. 


7. work 
countable – when talking about specific “works” of art, literature, etc. 
Hamlet is one of Shakespeare’s greatest works. 
uncountable – when talking about your general job or activities 
I have too much work to do.
