Lesson 45 – Substitution & Elimination 
There are some situations in which we eliminate or substitute words in order to 
make the sentence shorter. Today's lesson will teach you some of these "shortcuts" 
in the English language. 
one / ones / some 
To avoid repeating a countable noun, we can use one or ones: 
• “Does anybody have a pen?” 
“Here's one you can use.” 
• All the paintings are nice, but my favorite is the one of the ocean. 
• I thought the silver earrings looked best on her, but she bought the gold ones. 
• “Can you pass me those headphones?” 
“These?” 
“No, the other ones - the ones with the microphone attached.” 
To substitute uncountable nouns, we can use some: 
• “I need more paper.” 
“Here's some you can use.” 
• There's fresh-squeezed orange juice in the fridge. Do you want some? 
When using ones, we must define exactly which things we are talking about. If we 
do not define which things specifically, then we use some even for countable nouns. 
Compare these sentences: 
• I ate most of the cookies, but I saved some for my brother. 
• I ate most of the cookies, but I saved the chocolate ones for my brother. 
• You forgot to water the flowers, and now some are dead. 
• You forgot to water the flowers, and now the ones near the window are 
dead. 
We also do not use one/ones... 
1) After nouns that are used as adjectives: 
• I thought he was on the soccer team, but he's actually on the basketball team. 
the basketball one. 
• We visited an art museum and a science museum. 
a science one. 


2) After possessives (instead, just use the possessive ending in -S): 
• My calculator is broken; can I use yours? 
yours = your calculator 
• After I cut my hair, my sister wanted to cut hers to match. 
hers = her hair 
The word one/ones is optional in the following situations: 
1) After which 
• I have three screwdrivers; I'm not sure which (one) is the size you need. 
• You've tried on ten pairs of jeans. Which (ones) do you like the best? 
2) After superlatives 
• I've caught a lot of fish, but this is the biggest (one) I've ever seen! 
• I'm going to teach you several things. I'll start with the most important 
(one). 
3) After either, neither, each, another 
• Two of the houses by the lake are for sale, but neither (one) is within our 
price range. 
• She frosted the cupcakes and placed a cherry on top of each (one). 
• After he finished the book, he read another (one) by the same author. 
so / not 
We can use the word so to substitute part of a sentence, so that we don't have to 
repeat it. This is usually used with verbs involving thinking. It can also be used with 
say and tell (someone): 
• “Do you think I'll get the job?” 
“I think so. You have all the right skills.” 
= I think you’ll get the job. 
• “Did you pass all your final exams?” 
“I hope so. I haven't gotten my grades yet.” 
= I hope I passed the exams. 
• He thought my idea was brilliant, and said so. 
= said that my idea was brilliant 


• “You’re telling me the flight has been canceled?!” 
“I’m afraid so.” 
= I regret to inform you that the flight has been canceled.

For example: 
• She'll help me out. She promised she would. 
She promised so. 
• Has he ever been arrested? I doubt it. / I doubt he has. 
I doubt so. 
In negative sentences, we have two ways of saying it: using not, and using not + 
verb + so: 
• “Is the store open on Sunday?” 
“I don't think so.” 
• “It looks like the store isn't open.” 
“I guess not.” 
We typically use not + verb + so with believe, expect, imagine, think. 
• “Is it going to rain tomorrow?” 
“I don’t believe so.” 
We typically use not with be afraid, guess, hope, suspect. 


• “It won’t be possible, will it?” 
“I’m afraid not.” 
• “Is it going to rain tomorrow?” 
“I hope not.” 
do so / to 
We can use do so (or does so, did so, doing so, etc.) as a substitute for a verb 
phrase: 
• I wanted to paint my nails, but I didn't have time to do so. 
• The students promised to clean up, but they left without doing so. 
• He asked me to make the phone call and I did so immediately. 
• They walk their dog every morning; they've done so for the past five years. 
We typically use these with action verbs, not state verbs. 
In conversational English, we sometimes end a phrase with "to" without including 
the verb when it is clear from the context what action we are talking about. 
• We wanted to travel this month, but we won't be able to. 
• "Are you going to have any more kids?" 
"We're not planning to." 
The only time we cannot end with "to" is if the verb that follows it is "be." Compare 
these sentences: 
• She made way more food than she needed to. 
• The meeting was way longer than it needed to be. 
• "Do you ever go horseback riding?" 
"When I was younger, I used to." 
• "Are you a technician?" 
"I used to be. Now I'm a supervisor." 
Conversational deletion 
Finally, in informal English, you will sometimes hear yes/no questions being asked 
without the auxiliary verb: 
• Is everyone ready?  Everyone ready? 
• Are you hungry?  You hungry? 
• Did he get married recently?  He get married recently? 
• Have you ever been to Mexico?  You ever been to Mexico? 


Sometimes, when the subject of the sentence is you, the question is asked without 
either the auxiliary verb or the subject: 
• Are you hungry?  Hungry? 
• Do you want to join us?  Wanna join us? 
• Do you ever find yourself daydreaming?  Ever find yourself daydreaming? 
• Have you been thinking about traveling?  Been thinking about traveling? 
This is not grammatically correct, but it does sometimes happen in informal English. 
I’m not advising you to speak this way – I just want you to be able to recognize it 
when you hear it! 
Summary 
• We can use one/ones to avoid repeating a countable noun. 
o 
Does anybody have a pen? I need one. 
o 
I ate most of the cookies, but I saved the chocolate ones for my 
brother. 
• We can use some to substitute uncountable nouns or non-specific plural 
countable nouns: 
o 
This soup is delicious. Do you want some? 
o 
I ate most of the cookies, but I saved some for my brother. 
• Don't use one/ones after nouns that are used as adjectives or after 
possessives. One/ones is optional after which, superlatives, either, 
neither, each, and another. 
• The word so can be used after some "thinking" verbs to substitute part of a 
sentence. 
o 
"Did you pass your exams?" 
o 
"I hope so." 
• To make the negative, we can do it in two ways: 
o 
I don't think so. 
o 
I hope not. 
• Use do so as a substitute for an action verb phrase.  
o 
I wanted to paint my nails, but I didn't have time to do so. 
• We sometimes end a phrase with "to" without including the verb when it is 
clear from the context what action we are talking about. The only exception is 
when the verb after it is "be." 
o 
She made way more food than she needed to. 
o 
The meeting was way longer than it needed to be. 


• In informal English, auxiliary verbs and the subject "you" are sometimes 
omitted from yes/no questions: Are you hungry?  You hungry? / Hungry?
