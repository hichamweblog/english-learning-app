What’s the Deal with Tipping? 
 
Tipping service workers is a common practice in 
certain American industries. It is a way to show 
appreciation – or, for that matter, express your 
grievances – with the quality of service. There is 
some controversy over whether or not to tip, 
and although tipping is supposedly voluntary, 
the social pressure makes it feel like a must.  
 
The custom of tipping arose shortly after the 
Civil War, and was adopted from Europe. 
Americans with the means to travel abroad 
brought the ritual back with them, and flaunted 
the habit to demonstrate their status. Tipping 
was initially looked down upon as inconsistent 
with an egalitarian society. But in the early 20th 
century, the prohibition on alcoholic beverages 
caused hotels and restaurants to lose revenue, 
and proprietors began to welcome tips from 
customers.  
 
The most common setting for tips is in 
restaurants, where diners typically add an extra 
fifteen to twenty percent of the total bill as 
gratuity. The reason for such a sizeable tip is 
because waiters and waitresses often earn a 
pittance – they make far less than minimum 
wage; sometimes as low as just a couple dollars 
an hour. The workers are therefore banking on 
receiving tips in order to make a decent living.

In Europe, where the practice of tipping 
originated, waiters are paid higher wages and 
receive only small tips, such as leftover change – 
not to contribute to the bulk of their income, 
but simply as a gesture of goodwill. However, in 
these countries, the cost of employing waiters is 
built into the cost of the food.  
 
If tips were abolished in the U.S., the prices on 
the menu would have to rise in order to 
compensate for the costlier payroll – likely 
causing the restaurant to lose customers. 
Tipping is so entrenched in American culture 
that few restaurant owners are willing to stick 
their necks out. 
 
There is, however, an experimental movement of 
no-tip restaurants. Girard, a French restaurant in 
Philadelphia, prohibits tipping and pays its 
servers thirteen dollars an hour (more than 
twice minimum wage). The owners admit they 
forfeit some personal income to maintain this 
policy, but they are adamant that it pays off in 
terms of a better atmosphere for both diners and 
servers.

At takeout restaurants and cafes, there is a 
growing trend of setting a tip jar on the counter. 
Some Americans don’t mind dropping their 
change in the jar, but others find it to be a crude 
call for more money than deserved.  
 
Many people wonder where we draw the line 
for what type of worker should be tipped. After 
all, isn’t every employee, regardless of industry, 
providing a service to someone else? If we tip 
our manicurist, why not our dental hygienist? If 
we tip food delivery people, should we tip our 
mailmen? There isn’t a hard and fast rule for 
gratuity, so confusion sometimes evolves into 
bitterness.
