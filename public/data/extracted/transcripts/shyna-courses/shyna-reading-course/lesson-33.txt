The Psychology of Advertising 
 
The psychology of advertising goes beyond 
just a catchy tune and colorful product 
packaging. It taps into the area of the brain 
where humans can be influenced without 
being cognizant of the fact that we’re being 
persuaded.  
 
The visual aspects are the most prevalent – 
for example, the subtle placement of 
attractive young women in the background 
of a product for men, or the slanted ‘e’s in 
the Heineken beer logo that simulate a 
smile. These advertisements are 
meticulously engineered to 
subconsciously hook you and make you 
want the products.  
 
In the book entitled Hidden Persuasions, 
Marc Andrews, a psychologist and creative 
director from Amsterdam, works with two 
social psychologists to pry into many of the 
psychological ploys that are used in 
advertising. Marketing gurus design 
advertisements not only to plug the 
benefits of the product itself, but also to 
evoke an emotional response. The goal is to 
make consumers want the item so badly 
that the product goes from a “want” to a 
“need.”

Vocabulary 


 
 
anthropomorphism – altering a common 
object so that it appears to be more human-
like.  We have all seen examples of this in 
television commercials that feature 
everything from dancing laundry detergent 
to talking cereal. Although such tactics 
might seem silly, they are designed to help 
us bond with the brand or product, making 
us more likely to buy it. 
 
Another strategy involves linking a product 
with something intangible that we all 
yearn for. A washing machine, for example, 
can be presented as not just an appliance – 
but instead, as an integral part of caring for 
one’s family. Although you might not be 
immediately prompted to buy a washing 
machine by such a commercial, the 
associations linger in your mind when it’s 
time to make a buying decision. 
 
Our desire for social acceptance represents 
another vulnerability for advertisers to 
exploit. When phrases like “9 out of 10 
people prefer this product” or “1 million 
happy customers” are used, this triggers 
both our longing to be part of a group and 
our fear of being the odd one out. Although 
we might pride ourselves on our 
individuality, social proof in advertising 
actually makes us more likely to jump on 
the bandwagon.

Each of these tricks is part of the game of 
advertising psychology – and marketing

their sleeve. In fact, they usually design 
various versions of an advertisement, then 
run trials with groups of people in order to
