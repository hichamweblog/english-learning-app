Rejection Therapy 
Rejection – the word itself makes us 
cringe. When we’re rejected, we often 
blush, stammer, and feel like a complete 
failure. Rejection stings, and it can be a 
huge blow to our self-esteem. But is it 
possible to turn the tables? One young 
man named Jia Jiang made this his mission. 
 
Jia wanted to be an entrepreneur and 
change the world. He immigrated to the 
United States from China, graduated from 
college, got a stable corporate job, and got 
married. He was a textbook example of a 
success story – but deep down, he felt like 
something was missing. 
 
With his wife’s support and 
encouragement, Jia quit his job to pursue 
his dream of entrepreneurship just a few 
days before the couple’s first baby was 
born. It was a leap of faith, and there was 
a lot at stake.  
 
For four months, Jia worked on the 
prototype of his product and began 
seeking funding. He wanted to succeed so 
badly he dreamt about it five times. 
However, investors ultimately said “no” to 
his venture. There was no explanation and 
no opportunity for discussion.  
 
Jia was crushed. He wanted to keep trying, 
but the fear of experiencing another

rejection had paralyzed him. This prompted Jia 
to search online for ways to overcome it – and 
he came across something called “Rejection 
Therapy.” This is the idea that experiencing 
repeated rejections can help you become 
tougher.  
 
Jia decided to make 100 deliberate attempts to 
get rejected over the course of 100 days. He 
posted videos and write-ups on his blog so that 
he could be publicly accountable for 
completing the challenge. 
 
Some of the tasks he chose seemed silly – like 
asking for a free “burger refill” at a fast-food 
restaurant, or dancing in front of a security 
camera. Others were bound to create awkward 
social situations, such as joining a complete 
stranger’s party or asking passers-by to rate his 
looks on a scale from 1 to 10. And a few really 
took guts, like his requests to feed a lion, fly a 
plane with no experience, and interview 
President Obama. 
  
As Jia diligently tried task after task, he began 
to view the concept of rejection differently. It 
occurred to him that President Obama won the 
election with 51% of the vote – but that 49% of 
the votes had been against him. Jia began to 
ponder how various people dealt with rejection 
without being crippled by fear. Each time Jia set 
out to do a new challenge, he would brace 
himself for the rejection – but the results were 
surprising even to him.

Amidst a sea of ‘no’s’, there were a few people 
who said ‘yes’. When Jia knocked on the door 
of a random stranger to ask if he could play 
soccer in his backyard, the gentleman said 
‘come on in!’ Jia asked to drive a police car as 
if he were a cop – and the officer let him do it.  
 
One of the most remarkable experiences was 
when he went into a donut shop and made a 
special request to have donuts customized to 
look like the Olympic rings. The manager, 
Jackie, painstakingly created the set of 
donuts for him – and then said that they were 
on the house.  
 
Jia began to realize that there were many 
people who would agree even to a seemingly 
preposterous request. There was a human 
element that transcended the experience, 
providing small glitters of hope throughout 
the 100 days. Instead of dwelling on the 
rejections, Jia began to revel in the ‘yes 
moments.’  His fear of being rejected was 
superseded by the hope of finding the next 
open-minded stranger who would say “yes.”  
 
After his video of the donut incident went 
viral, Jia began receiving e-mails from fans 
who were inspired by his courage. By sharing 
his story, he has now become a successful 
public speaker. His quest to overcome 
rejection ended up jump-starting his career, 
enabling him to achieve his dream of changing 
the world.
