Parenting Styles Around the World 
It’s a given that all parents want what's best for 
their children. But depending on their culture, 
“what’s best” could mean wildly different things.  
Some parents try to keep their kids safe at all 
costs, while others cultivate independence in 
their offspring. There are parents who prioritize 
academic achievement, and other parents who 
just want their children to be healthy and happy, 
regardless of their levels of success. 
It's not just language and food that separate these 
different countries. Let's take a look at how their 
parenting styles differ too.  
Western Europe 
While French parents can seem fairly laid back 
when it comes to parenting, they're actually 
pretty strict. You might not see them fretting 
over every little thing their child does, but respect 
for adults is a non-negotiable. The French also 
take a hands-off approach and expect their kids 
to be independent from an early age.  
Meanwhile, the sociable Spaniards place a huge 
amount of importance on spending time with 
family. Kids who despise early bedtimes would 
love Spain, where parents let their children stay 
up late in order to be with their loved ones.  
British parents take a fairly democratic approach 
to child-rearing. Instead of simply punishing a 
troublesome child in a knee-jerk reaction, they'll

Scandinavia 
Norwegian mothers enjoy a generous amount 
of maternity leave – up to a year at 80% pay. 
After a child’s first birthday, he or she spends a 
good portion of the day in daycare. The 
daycare centers are subsidized by the state in 
order to encourage parents to return to the 
workforce.  
In both Norway and Denmark, there is an 
obsession with getting kids as much fresh air 
as possible. Danish parents will park their 
strollers outside the stores while they shop. 
Meanwhile, Norwegian parents often have 
children take their daily nap outside, even if 
it's snowing.  
Africa 
In Kenya and the Democratic Republic of the 
Congo, nobody bats an eye at the idea of 
sharing breastmilk. Most moms carry their 
babies on their backs in a sling, and it’s 
culturally acceptable to nurse anytime and 
anywhere.  
Tight-knit families and communities play a 
major role in caring for children. Occasionally, 
children will go to live with another relative or 
even another family if their own parents are 
overburdened. There is an ethos of shared 
responsibility when it comes to childcare.

South Koreans are similar in that parents are 
very involved with their children’s lives and 
push them to succeed. The parent-child 
relationship is more formal in Asian countries, 
and even adult children must defer to their

Japanese children have a high degree of

children to ride the subway by themselves 
even as young as five. Parents tend to 
emphasize being considerate of others and not 
rocking the boat. 
United States 
Parents in the United States highly value self-
expression and individuality – yet many have 
qualms about letting their kids out of their 
sight. One common theme in the U.S. is trying 
to be the child’s friend rather than an authority figure. 
American parents seem to have a lot of variety and different influences in how 
they raise kids. That's not surprising, considering the population's diverse 
backgrounds.
