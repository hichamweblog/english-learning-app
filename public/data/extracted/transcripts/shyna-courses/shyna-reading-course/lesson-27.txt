Three Fascinating Shipwrecks 
 
There is something about a shipwreck 
that captivates us. We wonder what led 
to the ship’s demise, and envision 
everything from pirates to espionage. 
The United Nations estimates that there 
are around three million shipwrecks 
littering the ocean floors. Here are a few 
of the interesting stories behind them: 
 
The American Star 
 
The American Star was built in 1940 and 
was used to transport both troops and 
civilians for 25 years. The vessel then 
changed hands many times, eventually 
falling into disrepair after years of 
neglect. It was sold for scrapping in the 
1980s, but a company purchased it with 
the intention of converting it into a five-
star hotel.  
 
However, while the American Star was 
being towed, a thunderstorm caused it 
to run aground in the Canary Islands. 
The strong waves pummeled the ship so 
forcefully that it broke in two, and the 
American Star was written off as a total 
loss and abandoned. Over the next 
fourteen years, the wreck slowly 
succumbed to the sea and now nothing 
remains but a few pieces of twisted steel.

The “Black Swan Project” 
 
Odyssey Marine Exploration is an American 
company that salvages valuable items from deep-
water shipwrecks. In 2007, they hit the jackpot 
with the discovery of a wreck off the coast of 
Portugal containing gold and silver coins estimated 
at $500 million. Odyssey code-named the operation 
“the Black Swan Project” and remained secretive 
about the exact location of the dive site. 
 
The treasure was initially thought to be from the 
English ship Merchant Royal, which sank in 1641 
with a heavy cargo of silver and gold. But the 
Spanish government disputed this, claiming that 
the wreck was actually a Spanish vessel called 
Nuestra Señora de las Mercedes. This ship was 
bringing treasure from South America to Spain 
when it was intercepted by the British and sunk 
during the ensuing battle. 
 
This caused a lengthy legal battle in American 
courts, during which the coins were closely 
examined and Odyssey was forced to disclose the 
location of the wreck. It was finally determined that 
the shipwreck and its treasure rightfully belonged 
to Spain. The coins were transported to Alicante, 
where they are now on display at a museum.

On July 25, 1956, the Andrea Doria was 
approaching New York on a foggy night. Another 
ship, called the Stockholm, was departing. 
Misinterpretation of radar signals and poor 
visibility created a recipe for disaster, and the two 
ships collided. 
 
Fortunately, the Andrea Doria's safety features 
enabled it to stay above water long enough for 
1,663 people on board to be rescued (57 died as a 
direct result of the collision). The ship took a full 11 
hours to sink, and the world watched the whole 
situation unfold on live TV. After this disaster, 
flying became the preferred method of trans-
Atlantic travel. 
 
The shipwreck itself is known as "the Mount 
Everest of scuba diving," since the hazardous 
conditions require immense skill and experience 
to navigate. There are strong currents and an 
invisible web of thin fishing lines that can ensnare 
divers, sixteen of whom have lost their lives while 
exploring the wreck.
