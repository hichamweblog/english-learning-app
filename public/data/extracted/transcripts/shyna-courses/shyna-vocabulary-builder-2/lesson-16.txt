Lesson 16: Philosophy & Worldview 
The word worldview refers to the sum total of all your beliefs, knowledge, 
understanding, philosophy, and values. Your worldview is shaped and 
influenced by your culture, your religion (or lack thereof), and your 
upbringing – as well as your own ideas and ideals. 
In today’s lesson we’re going to learn some general terms for talking about 
different worldviews and their elements. 
Worldview Basics 
One of the most basic divisions that influences peoples’ worldview is belief or 
disbelief in a higher power.  
• Religions like Christianity and Islam are monotheistic – they believe in 
only ONE god.  
• Religions like Hinduism are polytheistic – they believe in many gods 
and goddesses.  
• Some tribal religions are pantheistic – believing that nature or the 
universe is god, and god is inside everything in creation.  
• The belief that there is no god is called atheism, and someone who 
believes this is an atheist. 
Instead of a god, some people believe in fate or destiny – an impersonal force 
that controls everything in the universe – and that events are predetermined 
or predestined to occur. Others disagree, arguing that humans have free will 
– the ability to choose their actions independently. 
When someone officially adopts a religion, they have converted. Organized 
religions have doctrines or tenets – specific principles held as true. One word 
to describe the entire set of these principles is dogma – but this word 
sometimes has a negative connotation, often used to refer to principles that 
are accepted without thinking, questioning, or doubting.

There is no perfect religion or philosophy; you’ll often find contradictions or 
paradoxes – apparently impossible situations/beliefs that are still 
supposedly true. And at the root of all belief systems are certain underlying 
assumptions – things that are assumed to be true without evidence or proof.  
Some people think that there are no real philosophical certainties; it’s all 
conjecture – inferences and conclusions from incomplete evidence. 
The belief that moral and ethical “truths” are relative, and depend on the 
situation and people involved, is called relativism. 
The Most Common -ISMs 
A lot of philosophical ideas end in –ism, like atheism and relativism as you 
learned earlier in this lesson. There are hundreds of “isms”, but I’m going to 
teach you the most common ones. 
With each of the “isms,” you’re learning three words in one. Let’s take 
optimism as an example – the tendency to dwell on the positive: 
• optimism (n.) = the philosophy, the belief 
• optimist (n.) = a person who holds that belief 
• optimistic (adj.) = the descriptive adjective 
an optimistic article, an optimistic person, an optimistic view 
You can use these endings, –ist for the person and –istic for the adjective, for 
all the –ism words you’re about to learn. By the way, do you know what the 
opposite of optimism is? It’s pessimism – dwelling on the negative. 
One major dichotomy – a strong division between two contrasting things – is 
between realism and idealism. An idealist envisions things in an ideal or 
perfect manner, and thinks that our beliefs can change reality. A realist is 
more pragmatic (practical, dealing with real facts) and focuses on what 
actually exists in reality, not an imaginary version.

Humanism is a belief that rejects religion, 
but places a high value on humans and their 
capacities and worth. Anybody, whether 
religious or not, is capable of altruism – that 
means unselfish concern for the well-being 
of others.  
Some cultures and societies place a very 
high value on living together in community 
and depending on each other, whereas 
others emphasize individualism, a focus on the self-reliance and 
independence of each individual. 
Hedonism is the belief that pleasure and the pursuit of pleasure is the 
primary goal – especially sensual pleasures like eating, drinking, and sex.  
Pacifism is the belief that conflicts should always be settled peacefully, 
without violence and war. Feminism is the belief in the equality of men and 
women. 
Belief and Doubt 
Are you gullible? People who are gullible believe everything they read/hear 
without investigating or considering if it is really true. This type of person can 
be easily influenced and easily deceived.  
Or are you skeptical? A skeptical person doubts and questions everything, 
especially established facts. A skeptical person is difficult to persuade or 
convince. 
Another type of person is a fanatic – this is someone who has a strong and 
extreme enthusiasm for a certain belief or cause. Some fanatics have radical 
beliefs – the word “radical” means a very large departure from what is 
traditional or customary.
