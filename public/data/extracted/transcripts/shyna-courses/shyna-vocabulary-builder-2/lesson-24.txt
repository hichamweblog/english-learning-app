Lesson 24: Suffixes – Part 1 
Suffixes are added to the ends of words, usually to change their grammatical 
function or part of speech. Here’s an example of how one word can take many 
different forms depending on the suffix: 
• Tolerate = verb 
• Tolerance = noun 
• Tolerable = adjective meaning able to be tolerated 
• Tolerant = adjective describing a person who has this quality 
• Tolerantly = adverb meaning “in a tolerant way” 
Today’s lesson will teach you some more advanced suffixes for adjectives, 
adverbs, and verbs – we’ll leave the noun suffixes for tomorrow’s lesson. 
 
Adjective Suffixes 
The suffixes -able/-ible mean “is” or “can be.” This is easy to remember, 
because they’re similar to the word “able”! 
• washable = can be washed 
• preferable = is preferred 
• indispensable = essential (cannot be dispensed with) 
• bio-degradable = can be broken down by natural causes 
• convertible = can be converted 
• irrepressible = cannot be repressed 
• susceptible = easily affected, vulnerable 
• invincible = cannot be defeated/conquered 
The suffix –y is often added to nouns to describe something that has the same 
qualities:

• You already know that you can describe weather as sunny, windy, 
rainy, or foggy 
• This ending is often used with the tastes and textures of foods. 
Something that is milky is like milk in its color, taste, or texture. Foods 
can also be fruity, nutty, sugary, oily, salty, or fatty. 
• If someone is bossy, it means they are controlling in an annoying way – 
like they are trying to be your boss. 
• Something that is tricky is difficult and/or deceptive. 
Another suffix used to make comparisons is –like. 
• During this course, you’ve already learned the words lifelike (a lifelike 
painting) and childlike (someone with the attitude/temperament of a 
child). 
• The –like suffix is often used when you don’t know exactly what 
something is. Since you can’t name or describe it specifically, you make a 
comparison:  
o This thing is made of a rubber-like substance. 
o I saw a shark-like fish in the water. 
(You can add –like to virtually any noun, usually with a hyphen). 
The suffix –ful means “full of” and the suffix –less means “without”: 
• Someone who is careful has lots of care, and someone who is careless 
has no care. 
• If something is meaningful, it is full of meaning and emotionally 
significant; the opposite is meaningless. 
Some adjectives have only one form (either –ful or –less): 
• A person, comment, or expression that is scornful is full of scorn 
(contempt for something considered bad or inferior) – but there is no 
such word as “scornless.” 
• Someone or something that is nameless has no name, or its name is 
unknown or cannot be mentioned – but there is no such word as 
“nameful.”

The suffix –ish is also used to say something has that quality (often just a little 
bit): 
• reddish: somewhat red 
• greenish-yellow: yellow with a little bit of green 
• boyish/girlish: a little bit like a boy/girl 
• sheepish: embarrassed, meek, stupid 
A number of adjectives end in -ic / -ical, such as strategic (has strategy), 
emphatic (with emphasis) and biological (involving biology). 
When the noun already ends in –ic, the adjective becomes –ical: 
• magic/magical 
• critic/critical 
• logic/logical 
• music/musical 
• skeptic/skeptical 
A few words have differences between the –ic form and the –ical form: 
• historical = anything that happened in history 
• historic = something important in history 
• economic = related to the economy 
• economical = money-saving 
The suffix –most is used for direction – namely, the farthest point in a certain 
direction: 
• innermost/outermost 
• topmost/uppermost/bottommost 
• leftmost/rightmost 
• northernmost/southernmost/easternmost/westernmost 
(Ex. Alaska is the northernmost state in the United States) 
Finally, there are a few adjectives that end in –some, which means “inclined 
to” or “causing”:

• Something that is tiresome makes you tired. 
• Something that is worrisome makes you worried. 
• Someone who is winsome is charming, wins you over and makes you 
like them easily. 
• Someone who is meddlesome tends to meddle – to interfere, usually in 
things that are none of their business. 
 
Adverb Suffixes 
You probably already know that most English adverbs end in -ly (quickly, 
directly, beautifully) – but if the adjective already ends in –y, then the adverb 
form ends in –ily: 
• easy / easily 
• voluntary / voluntarily 
• hasty / hastily 
(quickly, in a rush) 
The suffix –ward means “in that direction”: 
• upward/downward 
• inward/outward 
• forward/backward 
• northward/southward/eastward/westward 
Some people add an –s (upwards, downwards, etc.) – this is acceptable 
informally, but the most typical form is without the –s. 
 
Verb Suffixes 
The most common verb suffix is -ate:  
• imitate: try to act/look exactly like someone/something 
• vaccinate: give a vaccine (against a disease) 


 
 
- EspressoEnglish.net 
• stagnate: become stagnant (no change, progress, improvement) 
• obliterate: completely destroy 
For verbs ending in –ate, the noun form ends in –ation: imitation, vaccination, 
stagnation, obliteration. Note the change in the word stress:  
• IM – i – tate (v.) 
• i – mi – TA – tion (n.) 
• ob – LIT – er – ate (v.) 
• ob – lit – er – A – tion (n.) 
The second most common verb suffix is -ize:  
• categorize: put into categories 
• destabilize: make unstable (the prefix “de” means “not”) 
• familiarize: make familiar 
• traumatize: cause emotional trauma 
For verbs ending in –ize, the noun form ends in –ization: categorization, 
destabilization, etc. Again we have a change in the word stress: 
• de – STA – bil - ize 
• de – sta – bil – i – ZA - tion 
A slightly less common verb ending is -fy:  
• identify, specify, beautify 
• fortify: make strong 
• stupefy: confuse, astound 
• mollify: to calm in temper/feeling, soothe 
• demystify: make less mysterious 
For verbs ending in –fy, the noun form ends in –fication: identification, 
specification, demystification – and the word stress also changes: 
• FORT – i – fy 
• fort - i – fi – CA - tion

Finally, we have a few verbs that end in -en: 
• Changes in color: brighten/darken/whiten/blacken 
• Changes in texture or shape: 
harden/soften/thicken/flatten/straighten 
• A few changes in feeling: gladden/sadden/sicken
