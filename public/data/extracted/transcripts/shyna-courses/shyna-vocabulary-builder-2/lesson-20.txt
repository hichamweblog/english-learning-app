Lesson 20: Connotations 
The English language is exceptionally rich in vocabulary. Although many 
words express the same or a similar concept, they often have different 
connotations.  
Connotations are the cultural or emotional associations that words bring to 
your mind. Connotations can be positive, negative, or neutral – and the 
selection of one word over another can give a particular tone to your text or 
communicate additional meaning “between the lines.”  
For example, if you are describing a person who is confident, direct and not 
afraid to go after what he wants, you can say he is “assertive” (this implies his 
confidence/directness is a good quality, and shows initiative) or that he is 
“pushy” (this implies that his confidence/directness is a bad quality, because 
it is annoying and he wants to control everything). 
Consider the various shades of meaning for synonyms of the word “thin,” 
which is the opposite of “fat.” The word “thin” has a neutral connotation. 
However, you can also describe someone as: 
• skinny – This implies that they are too thin, or underweight. For 
example, your mother might say, “You should eat more – you’re too 
skinny!” 
• scrawny – This implies a lack of muscle. A girl might say about a guy, 
“I’m not really attracted to him – he’s so scrawny. I prefer athletic guys.” 
• wiry / lean – This means thin and muscular; probably someone who 
does a lot of physical activity and is strong but not bulky. 
• slim – Thin with a positive connotation – thin and attractive. 
• slender / svelte – Thin and graceful/beautiful. “Svelte” adds an extra 
touch of being fashionable/sexy. 
• petite – Thin and short 
• lanky – Thin and tall

• gaunt – Thin and bony, as if the person is sick or undernourished (so 
their bones start to appear) 
• skeletal – So extremely thin that you can see a lot of bones, someone 
who is starving and possibly near death 
Connotations can be hard to master, because they are often determined by 
culture and common use – and they can differ from person to person. 
Sometimes you can understand them from the context. 
Consider these three sentences: 
• That hotel was a real bargain! We stayed for two whole weeks for less 
than $500. 
• I’m looking for an inexpensive hotel in Madrid. Do you have any 
recommendations? 
• We stayed in a cheap hotel next to the highway. It was noisy, the A/C 
was broken, and they ran out of coffee at breakfast. 
“A bargain,” “inexpensive,” and “cheap” all mean essentially the same thing – 
not costing very much money. But you can understand from the context that 
“a bargain” is described as a great thing, “inexpensive” is neutral, and “cheap” 
is used as a criticism of the hotel’s low quality. 
The Power of Words 
Connotations can be extremely politically and emotionally charged. 
Depending on your perspective… 
• The same person could be described as either a “terrorist” (negative) or 
a “freedom fighter” (positive) 
• The same event could be described as a “riot” (negative) or a 
“demonstration” (positive) 
• The same action could be described as “spying” (negative) or as 
“observing/monitoring” (positive)

Words are often intentionally chosen to make the reader/listener feel a 
certain way. One article, complaining about the large number of drug addicts 
on the streets, might call these people “druggies” or “junkies.” Another article, 
which wants us to feel more compassion, may call them “substance abusers” 
instead. 
Consider these different words for women: 
• lady – The most courteous and positive way to refer to a woman 
• woman – The normal, neutral word 
• girl – Some women get offended when men refer to mature women as 
“girls,” as if they were young. However, many women refer to their own 
groups of friends as “girls” or “gals.” 
• chick – This is a slang term, and can be positive or negative – “a hot 
chick” or “an ugly chick” – in either case, it is somewhat dehumanizing 
• bitch – Extremely offensive and derogatory word 
Can you see why it’s very important to know the connotations of the words 
you’re using?
