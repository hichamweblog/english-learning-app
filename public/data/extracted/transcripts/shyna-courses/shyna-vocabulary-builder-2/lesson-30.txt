Lesson 30: Beyond the Basics 
Today’s lesson aims to enrich your vocabulary by giving you additional 
options for talking about the basic concepts of size, space, time, weight, 
distance, speed, and quantity. 
Size / Space 
Something that covers a large area, usually outdoors, can be described as 
extensive or vast – extensive farmland, a vast desert – whereas a large 
enclosed space is spacious or roomy (a spacious apartment, a roomy car). 
One interesting word for something large is colossal, which comes from the 
same root as “coliseum” – this word can be used for both objects (a colossal 
cake, a colossal factory) and issues (a colossal problem, a colossal difference). 
Some interesting synonyms for “small” include compact (small and efficiently 
sized) – you would want a laptop for travel to be compact – and the informal 
word pint-sized, which is sometimes used to describe children and people of 
short stature. If your 5-year-old daughter dances, she’s a pint-sized ballerina. 
If something is very small, it is tiny, minuscule, or microscopic – that’s the 
smallest, as if you could only see it through a microscope. 
 
Time 
When something takes a very long time, we often exaggerate and say it takes 
ages or takes forever: “My internet connection is so slow that it takes ages 
to download a video.”

There are a number of informal ways to say something happens very fast: in a 
jiffy, in a sec, in a snap, in a flash, or the fastest of all: in the blink of an eye. 
Something that only lasts for a moment, and passes quickly, is fleeting: a 
fleeting glimpse (a quick look), a fleeting interest (a brief interest), a fleeting 
feeling. A more formal word for something that is not permanent is transient: 
for example, the transient beauty of a sunset, or transient workers who stay in 
one place briefly, then go somewhere else. 
The opposite – something that lasts a long time – can be described as 
persistent, protracted, or lingering. Here are some common collocations 
with each: 
• persistent problems, a persistent cough, a persistent rumor 
persistent = difficult to end or eliminate 
• a protracted discussion, a protracted battle, a protracted illness 
protracted = can be intentionally extended, as with discussions 
• lingering guilt, a lingering smell, lingering doubts 
Things that “linger” usually fade away on their own eventually 
Weight 
If something is heavy, large, and rather inconvenient, you can describe it as 
bulky, cumbersome, or unwieldy. The earliest cell phones were quite bulky 
compared to today’s tiny phones! 
Something light can also be described as lightweight. This is usually 
considered a good thing – you’d want to wear lightweight sneakers for 
running.

If something is light and lacking substance or stability, it is flimsy – this word 
has a negative connotation, like the thing might fall apart or collapse. 
Distance 
Two informal expressions for places that are very distant/remote are far-
flung and far-off: A far-flung corner of Russia, a far-off galaxy. 
 
A place that is distant from more populous locations can be described as out-
of-the-way: an out-of-the-way hotel 50 miles outside the city. If a place is 
extremely isolated, you can call it the middle of nowhere. 
Words for two things that are next to each other are neighboring and 
adjacent. France and Spain are neighboring countries; the cemetery is 
adjacent to the church. 
For two places that are near each other (but not immediately next to each 
other), we can say one is just around the corner or within a stone’s throw 
of the other. The bank is within a stone’s throw of the school. 
Movement / Speed 
One creative way to describe high speeds is to say something is lightning fast. 
When something – like a disease, a trend, or an idea – spreads very fast, you 
can say it’s spreading like wildfire.

The word careen implies rapid and out-of-control movement. It is often used 
for vehicles – a truck whose brakes have failed would careen down a 
mountain road. 
Most of the alternative words for “slow” have a negative connotation: 
something that is lagging is behind the schedule it should be following, and 
something that is sluggish or moving at a snail’s pace creates a mental 
picture of extreme and undesirable slowness. 
 
However, we do have the more positive word leisurely for “slow and relaxed” 
– a leisurely dinner, a leisurely walk. 
Quantity 
The English language has a great variety of ways to say “a lot.” When there are 
so many things that it’s impossible to count them all, we can use the word 
countless: The development of antibiotics has saved countless lives. 
One word used for abundant amounts is copious: copious rainfall, taking 
copious notes in class, drinking copious amounts of coffee. When there are 
many things of diverse types, we can use the adjective myriad: he has myriad 
skills (many different skills), I’ve read myriad books (various kinds of books). 
On the opposite end of the spectrum, when there is so little of something that 
it is hard to observe or measure, it is negligible: for example, an ineffective 
medicine that has a negligible effect on a disease. 
When an amount is small and rather pathetic, it can be called paltry: I can’t 
believe he sold his computer for a paltry $500! It’s worth at least twice that 
much.

Another word for something that lacks quantity is meager: A meager ten 
people showed up for the concert; when starting your career you’ll probably 
earn a meager salary.
