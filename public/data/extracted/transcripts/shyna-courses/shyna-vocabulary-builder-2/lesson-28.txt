Lesson 28: Non-Obvious Binomials 
Binomials are expressions with two words joined by a conjunction. Three 
binomials you probably already know are rock and roll, more or less, and 
step by step. 
With some binomials, it’s easy to figure out what they mean. If someone is 
sick and tired of a situation, it means they are frustrated with it. Talking 
about the ups and downs of an experience means the good moments (“ups”) 
and the bad moments (“downs”). 
This lesson will focus on binomials that are not so obvious – which may be 
difficult to understand based on their words alone. I’ve included some 
examples to better illustrate the use of these expressions. 
Binomials with AND 
• If something is growing or developing by leaps and bounds, it is 
progressing very fast. Both “leap” and “bound” refer to a big jump 
forward. 
 
• The expression every nook and cranny of a place means every possible 
tiny space in that location. If you lost your keys, you would look in every 
nook and cranny of your house in order to find them!

• Pins and needles refers to that tingling sensation felt in your hand or 
foot after it loses circulation. This expression is also used 
metaphorically – if you are on pins and needles, it means you are in a 
state of excited anticipation. 
 
• If a situation is touch and go, it means it is in a delicate or risky state. If 
someone is very sick and in danger of dying, doctors would say their 
condition is “touch and go.” 
• The nuts and bolts of something are its basics – you might have a good 
knowledge of the nuts and bolts of English grammar. This expression 
comes from the fact that nuts and bolts are essential parts of a machine. 
 
• A bait and switch is a deceptive strategy where you present an 
attractive offer, but then when the person becomes interested, you 
change it or try to make them do something else. If a store advertises 
cell phones on sale for $10, but in reality you can only get the $10 cell 
phone if you sign up for a $75/month contract, that’s a bait and switch 
tactic.

Binomials with OR/NOR 
• The phrase take it or leave it is often said when presenting an offer 
that the other person must either accept or reject; there is no room for 
negotiation. If you’re selling a watch, and the buyer is pressuring you for 
a discount, you could say “The price is $100. Take it or leave it.” 
• Somewhat similar to that is no ifs, ands, or buts, meaning there is no 
room for negotiation after giving a command. Your boss might want a 
project finished by Friday, no ifs, ands, or buts. It means the deadline is 
not extendable and no excuses will be accepted. 
• If you say you will do something come hell or high water, it means you 
are extremely determined and you will do it no matter what – 
regardless of difficulties or challenges. 
• The expression sink or swim is used metaphorically to mean succeed 
or fail based on your own efforts or abilities. Many parents help their 
children with homework, but when their kids go to college, it’ll be “sink 
or swim” – they must succeed or fail in their own merit. 
Binomials with Alliteration 
Alliteration is the repetition of the same sound at the beginnings of words. 
• Tossing and turning is when you can’t sleep, often because of worry – 
you turn from side to side in bed, but you just can’t relax or get 
comfortable. 
• To hem and haw is to speak with lots of hesitations, saying “um” and 
“ah” a lot, often because you are uncomfortable with the topic or trying 
to avoid saying something directly. Your co-worker might hem and haw 
if you point out a mistake in his work and he tries to explain it. 
• If something is without rhyme or reason, it is completely disorganized 
or has no purpose. If you write a report with no rhyme or reason, it has 
no logical order.

• Feast or famine means that sometimes there is too much of something, 
and other times there is too little. If you are a freelancer and you are 
overworked one month, but have no projects the next, you could say 
“It’s feast or famine.” 
• When someone is ranting and raving, they are wildly shouting or 
complaining about something that angers them. Someone with very 
strong political views might rant and rave about people who hold the 
opposite beliefs. 
• Something that is tried and true is dependable, it has been tested and 
proven to be good. For example, if a recipe is tried and true, it means 
that many people have used it successfully to cook a great meal. 
• If something is part and parcel of an experience, it means it’s a 
necessary part of the experience and cannot be avoided. Sleepless nights 
are part and parcel of having a new baby. 
Rhyming Binomials 
• Hustle and bustle refers to confusion and chaos in a busy place. New 
York City has a lot of “hustle and bustle,” but a small town in the 
mountains does not. 
• If you wine and dine someone, it means you entertain them with a 
fancy and expensive meal. Businesses often wine and dine potential 
clients or partners.

• Doom and gloom refers to a depressing feeling that a situation is bad 
and not likely to improve. If you have a pessimistic friend, you would tell 
them to cheer up because life is not all doom and gloom. 
• When someone acts high and mighty, it means they behave as though 
they are superior to others. Some people who go to a prestigious 
university act all high and mighty because they attended a famous 
school. 
• Leaving someone high and dry means to leave them in a difficult 
situation without any help. If you’re traveling in a foreign country, a 
dishonest taxi driver might leave you high and dry in a completely 
unknown part of the city. 
• If you say “it’s my way or the highway,” you are requiring other people 
to do what you want, or else they will have to leave or be excluded.
