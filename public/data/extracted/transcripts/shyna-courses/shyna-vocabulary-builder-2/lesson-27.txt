Lesson 27: Academic Expressions 
Today you’ll learn a few of the typical phrases that tend to be used in more 
formal writing and presentation. The expressions in this lesson are especially 
useful for an academic context, although they may be used in business as well 
as in writing any type of formal article or report. 
Make sure to check the lesson text for links to an extensive academic phrase 
bank as well as the Academic Word List, a group of the 570 most commonly-
used words in academic publications. 
This lesson centers around ten words that appear frequently in academic 
papers. 
Argument 
In the context of academics, argument refers to a course of logical reasoning, 
not to a verbal conflict. 
• If an author presents a compelling argument, that means one that is 
powerful and convincing. 
• On the other hand, if the argument is weak in its evidence or logic, then 
it is a tenuous argument. 
• Problems with an argument are called flaws in the argument. 
• Presenting evidence to the contrary is countering or refuting the 
argument. The word “refute” is stronger, meaning to disprove the 
argument; show that it is not true. 
Claim 
A claim is a statement of what someone believes to be the truth – it may be 
correct or incorrect. 
• If there is ample support for a claim, it means there is a lot of evidence 
that shows it is true.

• In contrast, an unsubstantiated claim is one with little or no evidence. 
• When you say that someone’s claim is not correct, you are challenging 
the claim. 
Evidence 
• When there is irrefutable/overwhelming evidence to support a claim, 
it means the evidence is so strong that it is practically impossible to 
argue against it. 
• The opposite is scant evidence – very little evidence. 
• Anecdotal evidence is evidence from anecdotes – peoples’ stories and 
experiences. This may conflict with experimental evidence, which is 
more objective and directly observed. 
• If the evidence corroborates a claim, it means it supports and 
strengthens it. 
Grounds 
“Grounds” refer to the basis or foundation of an argument or belief. 
• After presenting your evidence, you can write, “On these grounds, we 
can conclude that…” 
• If you have reasonable grounds for a conclusion, it means there is 
enough evidence to draw the conclusion (even if the evidence is not 
100% perfect and complete). 
• Ground is also used as a verb: you can say someone’s “views are 
grounded on” (based on) a certain belief or assumption. 
Issue 
Issue is a synonym for a particular subject or matter. 
• When an issue is under scrutiny, it means it is being very carefully 
examined. 
• We say an issue is clouded when it is not clear; it is complex and some 
other factors are interfering.

• The expression an underlying issue refers to one that is at the “root” of 
some problem or situation, although it might not be obvious. 
Literature 
When referring to the literature in an academic/scientific context, we are not 
talking about fiction, but instead the previous publications on the topic. 
• A growing body of literature means that more and more articles and 
books are being published on the topic. 
• If the literature abounds with examples, it means there are MANY 
examples in past publications. 
• When there is a consensus in the literature, it means there is a general 
agreement among authors who have previously studied and written 
about the subject. 
Premise 
A premise is one of the basic points in an argument, often an assumption that 
something is true. 
• If you can infer something from the premises, it means that you can 
get to a logical conclusion starting from the basic points. 
• Sometimes arguments are flawed because they are based on faulty 
premises – premises that are false or problematic. 
Research 
• The verb we usually use with “research” is do, but in a more formal 
context we often say conduct research or perform research. 
• Research that is pioneering or ground-breaking makes new progress 
in an area. 
• The findings of the research are its results or conclusions. 
Study

In academic/scientific English, “study” is often used as a noun to refer to the 
project. 
• A preliminary study is one that is done early, before the main research 
is performed. 
• A feasibility study is research done to see if something is feasible 
(possible). 
• One common phrase used to introduce the objectives of a piece of 
academic writing is: “The present study aims to…” 
Note that study is countable, but research is not. You can say “a study,” but 
not “a research.” 
View 
The word “view” is used to refer to a perspective or opinion. 
• The prevailing view is the view held by the majority. 
• If people have divergent views, then their views are different; they 
contrast each other. 
• When you expound on a view, it means you explain it in lots of detail.
