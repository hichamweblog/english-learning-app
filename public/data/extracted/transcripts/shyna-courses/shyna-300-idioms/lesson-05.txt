Explanation 
#1 - around the clock = (C) – without stopping 
 
First, let me clarify one thing: the difference between a clock and a watch. 
Many English students confuse these two words. 
 
  
 
   CLOCK 
 
 
 
WATCH 
The "hands" of a clock go around continuously in a circle. So if you do 
something around the clock, you do it for 24 hours a day, without stopping. 
#2 - light-years away = (A) – far in the future 
 
Light travels very fast, and the total distance that light travels in one year is 
called a light-year - it's about 10 trillion kilometers. That's very far! So the 
idiom "light-years away" means that something is very far in the future. 
#3 - like clockwork = (B) – exactly at regular times 
 
When a clock is working correctly, it is accurate and regular. So if something 
happens "like clockwork," it is predictable because it occurs exactly at regular 
intervals of time. 


 
#4 - rush hour = (B) – the period of time with lots of traffic 
 
Every weekday has two times that are considered "rush hour" - in the 
morning from around 7-9 AM, and in the afternoon from around 5-7 PM, 
because a lot of people are going to or leaving work - so there are many cars 
on the road, and the traffic is often slow or stopped. 
 
#5 - in the nick of time = (B) – at the last possible moment 
before something bad happens 
 
This idiom is very old. In the past, a "nick" was a small cut in a piece of wood. 
It was a way to mark measurement, so it had to be very precise. So if 
something happens "in the nick of time," it happens at exactly the right 
moment - usually the last moment before something bad happens. Here's an 
example: 
My teacher said she wouldn't accept any homework after 5 PM. I turned in my 
assignment at 4:55 - in the nick of time! 
#6 - spur of the moment = (C) – suddenly and spontaneously 
 
People who ride horses often wear a metal piece called a "spur" on their 
boots. If you kick the horse with the spurs, the horse will start running very 
suddenly.  


 
So a "spur of the moment" decision or action is done quickly and 
spontaneously, without thinking a lot about it beforehand. 
#7 - only a matter of time = (A) – certain to happen 
 
An alternative form of this idiom is "only a question of time." Both of these 
idioms can be followed by "before" or "until": 
• "That company has been losing money for years. It's only a matter of 
time [before / until] it goes bankrupt." 
Another common expression that also means something is certain to happen 
is sooner or later: “If you don't correct your posture, you're going to have 
back problems sooner or later.” 
#8 - have time on your hands = (A) – have free time with 
nothing to do 
 
A similar idiom is "have time to kill."  
The difference is that "I have time on my hands" is used for free time in 
general, and "I have time to kill" is used for when you have a period of time 
with nothing to do before another scheduled event. For example: "I arrived at 
the theater much too early, so I had some time to kill before the movie 


 
started." (Or you can say specifically how much time: I had an hour to kill 
before the movie started) 
#9 - it made my day = (A) – it made me very happy 
 
If you say something "made your day," it means it made you so happy that it 
made the entire day a good one! 
#10 - as plain as day = (B) – obvious 
 
In the daytime, there is lots of light and it's easy to see - differently from the 
nighttime, when it's dark and difficult to see. So if something is "as plain as 
day," it means it's obvious and easy to see. 
 
#11 - it's about time = (B) – it should have happened already  
 
This phrase is often used when complaining or criticizing. For example, if 
you're in a restaurant, and you wait 45 minutes for a waiter to come to your 
table, when he finally comes, you could say, "It's about time we got some 
service!" - you are complaining that the service should have been provided 
earlier. 


 
#12 - having the time of your life = (B) – really enjoying an 
experience 
 
This phrase can be used in the past, present, or future. 
Past:  
"Our kids had the time of their lives at Disneyland last year." 
Use it in the present for an experience happening in the moment of speaking:  
"I'm at the show right now. I'm having the time of my life!" 
Future: 
"You're going to have the time of your life in New Zealand - it's a wonderful 
country." 
There are several other idioms with the same meaning, including "having a 
ball" and "having a blast."
