Lesson 19: Idioms with GET 
 
In the final section of this course, you’ll learn idioms using common English 
verbs. Our first verb is “get.” 
Introductory Quiz

B. travel with it

Explanation 
“Get a kick out of” (A) is an informal way to say you enjoy something. It’s not 
usually used for general activities like reading or watching movies, but rather 
for a specific thing that you thought was amusing, funny, or particularly 
enjoyable.  


“Get cracking” (C) means “start” – specifically starting some work on a 
project or task. This idiom is most commonly used in the present, especially in 
the phrase “let’s get cracking!” 
“Get the drift” (B) or “get the picture” means you understand the general 
idea, even if you don’t know all the details. Instead, you have an overall 
understanding of the situation. 
 “Getting the hang of something” (C) means you’re succeeding in learning 
how to do it. This is especially used for things that are a little bit difficult to 
learn, and take a long time to master. But if you’re getting the hang of it, then 
you’re beginning to do it well after some practice or training. 
“Get even with someone” (B) or “get back at someone” means to get 
revenge – after the person does something bad to you, you do something bad 
to that person in order to hurt them, because they hurt you. 
“Get a head start” (A) means to start early so that you have some advantage. 
We usually use this expression when there is a particular timeline for the 
work to be completed, and we do the work even earlier than necessary. 
To “get your bearings” (C) means to understand your position. This can be 
used for actual physical location, as in the example about getting your 
bearings in the city center. It can also be used to understand your situation in 
life – especially after something disorienting happens: “After my wife left me, I 
moved back to my hometown to get my bearings.”  
If you “get into the swing of things,” (A) it means you become familiar with a 
routine, activity, or situation so that you can participate in or enjoy it. We use 
this when the routine or activity is already in progress, and we are joining it in 
the middle, so it may take some time for us to get accustomed – to get into the 
swing of things. 
The expression “get back on track” (B) is used when a project or process has 
gone away from the normal or best path, but then comes back to it. 
If you “get a lot of mileage out of” (C) something, it means you find it very 
useful, or you will use it for a long time. This idiom comes from the fact that if 


a car has “good mileage,” you can drive many miles for the amount of gasoline. 
But the expression applies to using other items, not just cars.
