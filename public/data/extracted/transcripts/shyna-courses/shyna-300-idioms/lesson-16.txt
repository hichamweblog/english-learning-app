Explanation 
#1 - change your tune = (C) – change your opinion completely 
 
The word "tune" means the melody of the music. If you changed the tune of a 
song, then it would be a completely different song - so the idiom "change your 
tune" means to change your opinion or attitude completely - often when the 
new opinion/attitude will bring you some advantage. 
 
#2 - face the music = (B) – deal with the unpleasant 
consequences of your actions 
 
The exact origin of this idiom is unknown, but some people say it comes from 
an old military tradition of dismissing disgraced officers to the sound of 
drums.  
 


The idiom facing the music means to confront and accept the unpleasant 
consequences - such as criticism or punishment - resulting from something 
you did. 
#3 - fine-tune something = (B) – make small adjustments to 
improve it 
 
The word "tune" as a verb means adjusting musical instruments so that the 
sound is perfect. Musicians in an orchestra tune their instruments before a 
performance. This is why the word fine-tune can be used to describe making 
small adjustments to optimize something. 
 
#4 - fit as a fiddle = (A) – in great health 
 
A “fiddle” is an instrument like a violin. Musical instruments like this need to 
be kept in excellent condition in order to play well - so describing someone as 
“fit as a fiddle” means the person is in excellent health and excellent physical 
condition. 
#5 - play it by ear = (A) – decide how to deal with a situation as 
it develops 
 


Musicians who "play by ear" can play a song without looking at the written 
music - they simply hear the music and then play it. When playing by ear, they 
are also free to improvise, because they are not restricted to the written 
music.  
The idiom "play it by ear" therefore means to proceed without a fixed plan. 
Instead, you improvise your response to the situation as it develops. 
 
#6 - like a broken record = (C) – say the same thing repeatedly 
 
This idiom comes from the technology of the past! This large, black, vinyl disc 
is called a record. If there is a flaw in the record, it often plays the same small 
piece of music repeatedly. This is why a person who says the same thing 
repeatedly is compared to a broken record. 
 
#7 - singing its praises = (B) – saying lots of good things about it 
 
The word "praises" means compliments and positive statements. For example, 
in church, people sing praises to God.  


So, if you "sing someone's praises" or "sing something's praises," it means you 
say a lot of good things about that person or thing. 
 
#8 - play second fiddle = (B) – in a less important position 
 
This idiom also comes from the orchestra. In the past, the best violin or fiddle 
player was often considered the "star" of the orchestra, and the other violin 
players were secondary - that is, less important. So if you play second fiddle to 
someone, it means that you have a less important position or role than that 
person. 
#9 - chime in = (A) – add a comment to a discussion 
 
The word "chimes" refers to a type of instrument made of metal tubes. The 
chimes are usually not played alone; instead, they add a complementary 
sound to the music of the other instruments in the orchestra. This is why the 
expression "chime in" means to add your comment or contribution to a 
conversation or discussion that is already in progress. 
 


#10 - struck a chord = (A) – had a particularly strong effect  
 
A chord is a set of two or more notes that are played together. A chord needs 
to have the perfect combination of notes in order to create a pleasant sound. 
 
If something - for example, a book - "struck a chord" with you, it had the 
perfect combination of elements to result in a strong emotional effect - 
especially when it is connected to your life in some way. 
#11 - that's music to my ears = (C) – I'm very happy to hear that 
 
Listening to music usually makes people happy - so when you hear something 
that makes you happy, you can say "that's music to my ears!" 
#12 - pull out all the stops = (B) – make every possible effort 
 
This very large instrument, often found in old churches, is called a pipe organ.  
 
 


These parts of the instrument are called the "stops" - they control the flow of 
air in the pipes. If you pull out all the stops, you would completely liberate the 
flow of air so that the instrument would play at the maximum possible 
volume. 
The idiom "pull out all the stops" therefore means to make every possible 
effort, doing something with complete dedication and no hesitation, in order 
to make it a success.
