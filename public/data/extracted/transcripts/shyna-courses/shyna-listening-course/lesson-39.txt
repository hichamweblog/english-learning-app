Lesson 39: Junk Food 
Warm-Up: 
What are some of your “guilty pleasures” – junk foods 
that you know are bad for you, but you really enjoy? 
Have you ever tried to modify your diet? What were 
the results? 
 
 
 
 
Activity 1 – Pre-Learn Vocabulary 
willpower (n.) = the strength of your desire and ability to determine your actions 
Ex) It takes a lot of willpower to practice the piano every day for five hours – 
but that’s what you need to do if you want to become a professional. 
binge (n./v.) = consume an excessive amount of something – usually food or drink 
 
Ex) My kids binged on popcorn and candy at the circus. 
starve (v.) = suffer from lack of food. The expression “I’m starving” is also used as 
an exaggerated way to say you are extremely hungry. 
 
Ex) The abandoned dog was injured and starving; it hadn’t eaten in weeks. 
tactic (n.) = a practical strategy or method for achieving a goal 
 
Ex) Making a budget is one tactic for helping you manage your money better.  
crave (v.) = have an intense desire for something 
Ex) Although I’d already eaten dinner, I was craving some pizza, so I ordered 
one for delivery. 
dampen (v.) = reduce, make less intense 
Ex) The news that some of the activities had been canceled dampened our 
enthusiasm for the event. 


overindulge (v.) = eat more than the appropriate amount 
Ex) My mother’s homemade cookies are so delicious, it’s hard not to 
overindulge!  
melt (v.) = when something solid becomes liquid 
 
Ex) She left her glass of lemonade out in the sun, and all the ice cubes melted. 
myriad (n./adj.) = many and various 
Ex) The cultural center has myriad classes in dance, photography, art, and 
theater. 
misleading (adj.) = deceptive, leading someone to the wrong conclusion 
Ex) The wording on the advertisement is misleading – it implies that the 
service is free when it’s actually not. 
serving (n.) = when talking about food, one “serving” is a single portion, the normal 
amount for eating. (Another word for this is a “helping.”) 
 
Ex) I liked the mashed potatoes so much that I ordered another serving.

Complete Transcript 
If you just can’t seem to cut down on cookies, chips, and soda, it may not be entirely 
due to a lack of willpower on your part. The disturbing truth is that food companies 
have intentionally designed their products to be addictive. 
The addictive properties of junk food are illustrated in an experiment in which rats 
were allowed to binge on high-fat foods for several weeks. The junk food was then 
taken away and healthier food was given - but the rats preferred to starve 
themselves rather than eat the healthier food. Researchers then offered the rats a 
choice between eating junk food, which was punished with an electric shock, and 
regular food - and the rats still preferred the unhealthy option, regardless of the 
pain. 
Here are three examples of the sneaky tactics that are used to manipulate our taste 
buds and keep us hooked on food that is harmful to our health. One is something 
called the "bliss point" - the specific balance of flavors that maximizes your pleasure 
while still leaving you craving more. If there is too little taste, you won’t enjoy the 
food – but if the flavor is too strong, it will satisfy you and dampen your desire to 
overindulge. 
One brand of soda prepared 61 formulas with subtle differences and tested them 
with thousands of people until it gathered enough data to determine the perfect 
blend of ingredients - perfect for turning you into an addict, that is. The heaviest 
soda drinkers can consume upwards of 1,000 cans a year, resulting in million-dollar 
profits for the company.  
Chips and other puffy snacks take advantage of a phenomenon called "vanishing 
caloric density" - because they are light and easily melt in your mouth, your brain 
assumes there's no calories in them - and you can keep eating them forever. Of 


course, the chips are loaded with salt and fat, which contribute to high blood 
pressure and myriad health problems. 
Finally, misleading advertising and labeling practices can confuse even people who 
are trying to be health- conscious. Eighty percent of food sold in the United States – 
even food that is billed as “healthy” such as whole wheat bread – has added sugar. 
But you might never know it, because there are fifty-six different terms for 
processed sugar. So if you don’t see the word “sugar” on the label, it may be 
masquerading under a different name. 
Sometimes the packaging advertises a positive point to convince us we’re making a 
healthy choice – conveniently omitting the fact that unhealthy ingredients lurk 
within. The label "all natural " may be slapped on a product that is high in sodium, 
and the word "light" may mean the food has less fat, but the same number of 
calories. Many "fat free" foods are loaded with sugar, and snacks that claim to be 
"made with real fruit" may only include a tiny percentage of it.  
Perhaps the most insidious trick has to do with the standard serving size - to make 
the product look lower in calories, the nutritional information on the label is based 
on an unrealistically small serving. Most people eat two or three times that amount 
in one sitting – so the next time you’re looking at a label, remember to double the 
calories for the serving size listed.  
 
 
 
 
 
 


Extra Vocabulary 
"Here are three examples of the sneaky tactics that are used to manipulate our 
taste buds" 
The word “sneaky” means deceptive. Your “taste buds” are the sensors on 
your tongue that detect the sensation of different flavors. 
"Chips and other puffy snacks" 
 
Something that is “puffy” is light and full of air. 
"the chips are loaded with salt and fat" 
 
The word “loaded” in this context means the chips have a LOT of salt and fat. 
"even food that is billed as “healthy” such as whole wheat bread" 
The word “billed” in this context means advertised or presented in a certain 
way. 
"So if you don’t see the word “sugar” on the label, it may be masquerading under a 
different name." 
The verb “masquerade” means to be disguised, to pretend to be something 
you’re not. 
"conveniently omitting the fact that unhealthy ingredients lurk within" 
To “omit” something means NOT to include or mention it. The verb “lurk” 
means to exist in a hidden and threatening way. 
"Perhaps the most insidious trick has to do with the standard serving size" 
The adjective “insidious” means working to cause harm in a way that is not 
obvious. 
"Most people eat two or three times that amount in one sitting" 
If you do something “in one sitting,” it means you do it during one period of 
time without stopping.
