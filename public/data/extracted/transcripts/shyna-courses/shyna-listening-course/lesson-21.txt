Activity 1 – Pre-Learn Vocabulary 
jarring (adj.) = if you describe an experience as “jarring,” it means it is shocking, 
disturbing, and irritating 
Ex) The jarring contrast between the five-star hotel and the slums nearby 
makes many tourists uncomfortable. 
standoffish (adj.) = cold and closed-off, someone who doesn’t want to interact with 
other people 
Ex) The first time I met Jason, I thought he was a bit standoffish – he kept 
looking at his watch and he barely made eye contact with me. 
pastime (n.) = a pleasant way to pass the time 
Ex) My favorite family pastime is baking Christmas cookies together with my 
sisters. 
cut-throat (adj.) = extremely competitive 
 
Ex) Fashion is a cut-throat industry – only the very best models become famous. 
risk-averse (adj.) = tending to avoid taking risks; being unwilling to take risks 


Ex) People who are risk-averse don’t typically invest in the stock market. 
status quo (n.) = the currently existing status/situation 
Ex) The population is unhappy with the status quo and is demanding change. 
wary (adj.) = cautious and a little bit suspicious 
Ex) He’s not talking too much about his great business idea because he’s wary 
that a competitor will steal it. 
taboo (n./adj.) = forbidden by social custom 
 
Ex) In the U.S., it’s taboo to ask somebody how much money they make.

Complete Transcript 
As the famous playwright George Bernard Shaw once said, "England and America 
are two countries divided by a common language." Although the U.S. and the U.K. 
share a common history and language, the differences in business culture can be 
jarring for a businessperson going from the U.S. to the U.K. or vice versa. What is 
common, accepted, and even celebrated in one place may be seen as awkward or 
even unprofessional in the other. 
For one thing, work attire in the U.K. tends to be more formal than in the U.S. - so 
Americans would be well-advised to dress impeccably when meeting with British 
clients or colleagues. In both countries, it's common to have a few minutes of chit-
chat before getting down to business – though the topics of conversation are likely 
to differ. For Americans, small talk often centers on sports, celebrity gossip, and 
events in the U.S.; whereas the British usually chat about the weather and world 
news. 
Americans tend to be more overtly friendly and welcoming right away, even when 
interacting with perfect strangers - and the British may appear rather standoffish by 
comparison. However, relationships are extremely important in business in the U.K. 
- perhaps even more so than in the U.S. Americans often see "networking" as a 
means to an end - a way to get a promotion or find the best business deal. For Brits, 
on the other hand, the relationships themselves are an important indicator of 
success. Also, Americans tend not to socialize with their coworkers, preferring to 
spend time with their families or other circles of friends - but for Brits, having a 
drink with colleagues after work is a near-universal pastime. 
People who have worked in both countries describe the American business 
environment as far more intense and cutthroat. It is highly competitive and 
Americans tend to want everything done "yesterday." This urgency ends up blurring 
the lines of work-life balance; it is common for American employees to stay at the 
office late and check work e-mail even while at home or on vacation. In the U.K., 
business moves at a slower pace and there is no expectation of 24/7 availability. 
British companies tend to reward employees by offering more time off or more 
flexible hours, whereas American companies pay employees extra for working 
overtime. 
Although the American model puts more pressure on its employees, it is 
advantageous in terms of progress - American companies can innovate, adapt, and 
implement new ideas faster. A strong entrepreneurial spirit pervades the business 
culture of the U.S., whereas the British tend to be more risk-averse. British 


businesses typically seek to preserve the status quo, whereas American businesses 
often aim to disrupt it. One American in the U.K. commented that British businesses 
miss out on opportunities to make more money because they are wary of changing 
their traditional way of doing business. 
One of the deepest-running differences between American and British business 
cultures has to do with hierarchy. The U.K. is one of the oldest monarchies in the 
world, and the focus on social class and seniority is apparent in the business 
environment as well. By contrast, the U.S. has developed a culture with a strong 
emphasis on individual competency and achievement.  
This difference may be striking during meetings. American meetings often appear to 
be free-for-all brainstorming sessions, where employees of various levels of 
seniority freely contribute suggestions, and may even challenge their superiors’ 
ideas. This type of initiative and assertiveness would be out of place at British 
meetings, where there is a clear order and a socially-accepted time and place for 
each person to speak. 
Another result of the American exaltation of the individual is that in the U.S., self-
promotion is an art and skill for getting ahead in one's career. Americans aren't shy 
about highlighting their accomplishments or "selling themselves" in order to land a 
job, close a deal, or get a promotion. In the U.K., however, "tooting your own horn" is 
essentially taboo. Brits prefer to deflect compliments and downplay their own 
accomplishments rather than basking in the spotlight like Americans do. 
Finally, Americans and British have markedly different communication styles - and 
the differences go beyond spelling and pronunciation. Americans tend to be 
forthright with their thoughts and do not hesitate to offer criticism. They are also 
more likely to demonstrate strong emotion, such as enthusiasm, at work. The British 
are more reserved and indirect, often speaking in understatements such as 
describing a complete disaster by saying something like "things didn't quite go 
according to plan." Americans doing business in the U.K. must learn to "read 
between the lines" in order to understand what's really being said.  
When giving directions to their subordinates, British supervisors tend to phrase 
their directives in terms of polite requests or suggestions rather than direct 
commands – and this can certainly cause confusion in day-to-day interaction at 
work. In the U.K., a manager might say, “If you have time, you might want to look 
into that problem” – and an American employee would interpret this as being an 
optional and low-priority task. On the other hand, an American manager saying 
“Please find a solution for that problem right away” might rub a British employee 
the wrong way, as it would sound too blunt and even a little rude. 


Extra Vocabulary 
“a businessperson going from the U.S. to the U.K. or vice versa” 
“Vice versa” means “reversed” or “the other way around” – so in this sentence, 
it’s a short way of saying “going from the U.S. to the U.K. or the U.K. to the U.S.” 
“it's common to have a few minutes of chit-chat before getting down to business” 
“Chit-chat” is informal social conversation, without talking about anything 
serious 
“Americans often see ‘networking’ as a means to an end” 
“A means to an end” is an expression meaning “a way to achieve a different 
objective” – in other words, Americans often use networking in order to reach a 
certain goal, not because they value the activity of networking itself 
“This urgency ends up blurring the lines of work-life balance” 
 
“Blurring the lines” means making the boundaries and limits unclear. 
“American meetings often appear to be free-for-all brainstorming sessions” 
 
“Free-for-all” refers to a disorganized event without rules or order 
“In the U.K., however, ‘tooting your own horn’ is essentially taboo.” 
“Tooting your own horn” is an expression that refers to talking about yourself 
and saying great things about yourself. 
“Brits prefer to deflect compliments and downplay their own accomplishments 
rather than basking in the spotlight like Americans do.” 
To “bask in the spotlight” means to enjoy having everyone’s attention focused on 
you. 
“might rub a British employee the wrong way” 
To “rub someone the wrong way” means to irritate or annoy someone, often 
unintentionally.
