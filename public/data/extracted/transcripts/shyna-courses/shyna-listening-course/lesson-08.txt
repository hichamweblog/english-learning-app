Activity 1: Pre-Learn Vocabulary 
major (n./v.) = a university student’s area of concentrated study. It is the subject 
that the student studies most, and the student will receive a degree in this topic. 
This word can be used as either a noun (describing the subject OR the person who is 
studying it) or as a verb: 
 
As a noun: My brother is an economics major. 
 
As a noun: This program is an interdisciplinary major in biology and chemistry. 
As a verb: I was planning to major in journalism, but I changed my mind and 
ended up majoring in philosophy. 
 
fossil (n.) = remains of animals or plants from a 
very long time ago, which have been preserved in 
rock 
 
lab (n.) = short for “laboratory” – a place where 
science experiments are done 
 
Ex) The doctor sent the blood samples to the lab for testing. 


hands-on (adj.) = describes something that involves active participation, practical 
things, not just learning theory 
Ex) My teenage daughter loves working with animals. She just joined a program 
that will give her some hands-on experience at a veterinary clinic. 
undergrads (n.) = short for “undergraduates” – people who are studying in the 
regular college program to get a bachelor’s degree (typically four years). 
This is in contrast to “grad students,” who are studying in a more advanced program 
like a master’s or doctorate degree. 
 
Ex) Peter is an undergrad at Columbia University. He’s in his third year. 
algae (n.) = plants in the ocean. “Seaweed” is one common type of algae. 
 
second-guess (v.) = wonder/doubt if your ideas or decisions are really correct 
Ex) My parents raised me with very conservative beliefs, but when I left home I 
began to second-guess many of the things I’d been taught. 
sure-fire (adj.) = describes something that will definitely have a particular result 
(can be positive or negative) 
Ex) Elena has so many fans of her blog, that any book she publishes will be a 
sure-fire success. = will definitely have the positive result of succeeding 
 
Ex) Being late for a job interview is a sure-fire way to make a bad impression. 
 
= will definitely have the negative result of making a bad impression

Complete Transcript 


I was a chemistry major in college, and I picked that because I had always been 
interested in science as a kid. I was really into volcanos and geology, dinosaurs and 
fossils, and stuff like that, and I used to read some pretty advanced books for my age 
on those topics.  
Both of my parents have a science background - my dad was an engineer, and my 
mom has a psychology degree - so they always encouraged me, and my dad would 
do these science experiments for me and my brother to see and learn some of the 
principles of chemistry and physics and whatnot. 
Also in high school I had a wonderful chemistry teacher, she really made the subject 
fascinating, and not just like a boring list of chemical reactions and rules to 
memorize. We would have class with her, but we would also do lab work, you know, 
experiments and it was very hands-on. So when it came time to pick a major in 
college, it was a toss-up between chemistry and physics, but I eventually settled on 
chemistry. 
I went to a pretty small college, and what was great about that is that I had the 
opportunity to use lab equipment that normally undergrads wouldn't get a chance 
to use. We were trained to use some really sophisticated machinery and we were 
able to do research that is really quite similar to what we'd be doing if we 
continued into a career in chemistry.  
And I absolutely loved the process of discovery, of using what was already known 
to investigate things that were unknown. My area of specialization was marine 
natural products chemistry – so basically, studying sea creatures like sponges and 
algae, and trying to extract and identify some of the chemical compounds in them 
that had anti-bacterial and cancer-fighting properties, so that these could eventually 
be developed into new drugs and medicines. 
Both during and after my college years I did a couple of internships in a 
pharmaceutical company, which was doing the same kind of work, and it was there 
that I started to second-guess myself and question whether or not I really wanted 
to do this type of work for the rest of my life. Although the work was interesting, it 
was also very slow – it can take years to make progress in this area – and it was also 
extremely, extremely specialized, which felt limiting because I have a really broad 
range of interests.  


So I decided not to continue – I turned down a scholarship to go to grad school – and 
it was a tough decision because I was basically turning my back on a sure-fire 
career with a great starting salary, and instead choosing a path of uncertainty. But I 
know it was ultimately the right decision. 
Nowadays, now that people know me as an English teacher, they’re often pretty 
surprised to hear that I have a degree in chemistry – but I actually think my science 
background has helped me in some indirect ways. That ability to research and 
investigate means I’m always experimenting and testing to discover the best ways to 
help my students learn, and I’m always learning new things myself in the process.  
Also, in college I did a lot of presentations of my chemistry research, and so I 
learned how to communicate some very complicated concepts in layman’s terms, so 
that they would be clear and understandable to someone even if they didn’t have 
any knowledge of chemistry at all. And now it’s a fundamental characteristic of my 
teaching style, this ability to simplify what seems confusing and complex, and I can 
thank my experience in the field of chemistry for that, too. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Extra Vocabulary 
"Both of my parents have a science background" 


If a person has a “background” in a particular area, it means they have studied 
it or have experience in that subject. 
"chemistry and physics and whatnot." 
“…and whatnot” is an informal way to say “and other related things.” It is 
similar to “et cetera” or “etc.” 
 "it was a toss-up between chemistry and physics" 
A “toss-up” means an undecided choice, often one that is 
decided by chance. This expression comes from the 
tradition of “flipping a coin” (called a “coin flip” or “coin 
toss”) to decide things. 
"new drugs and medicines." 
The word “drug” in English can be used to describe either medical substances 
that are used to treat diseases OR illegal drugs (cocaine, heroin, etc.) You’ll 
know which meaning it has based on the context. 
"I turned down a scholarship to go to grad school" 
The phrasal verb “turn down” means to reject, or not to accept an 
opportunity. “Grad school” refers to more advanced university studies 
(master’s and doctorate degrees) 
"communicate some very complicated concepts in layman’s terms" 
A “layman” is someone who is not an expert in a particular area. The 
expression “in layman’s terms” means to say something in a way that an 
everyday person can understand, not using rare and complicated words that 
only a professional in the area would know.
