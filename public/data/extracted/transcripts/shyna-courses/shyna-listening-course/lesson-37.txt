Lesson 37: Best Pranks in History 
Warm-Up: 
A “prank” is a trick or joke, often one where you convince 
somebody to believe something that isn’t true, or make them 
react to a ridiculous situation. Have you ever played a prank 
or been a victim of a prank? Do you think pranks are mean, or 
just innocent fun? 
 
 
Activity 1 – Pre-Learn Vocabulary 
footage (n.) = recorded video 
 
Ex) I saw some footage of the riots on the 8 o’clock news. 
implausible (adj.) = difficult to believe because it is not likely/probable 
Ex) Some people claim they have been kidnapped by aliens, but I find their 
stories implausible. 
delicacy (n.) = a food that is especially fine (often because it is rare or expensive) 
 
Ex) At the banquet, they served caviar and other delicacies. 
dupe (v.) = if you dupe someone, it means you deceive them 
Ex) The boy duped his parents into believing he was sick so that he could stay 
home from school. 
gravity (n.) = the force of the planet Earth that pulls objects down 
Ex) Rockets need to use a lot of fuel in order to propel them upwards and escape 
the earth’s gravity. 
verbose (adj.) = using an excessive number of words, more words than necessary. 
The word “verbose” is a little older/more formal; in everyday speaking we usually 
use the term “wordy” to describe something with too many extra words. 


Ex) My history teacher is quite verbose. I wish she’d get to the point a little 
faster. 
tactless (adj.) = “tact” is politeness and consideration, so “tactless” means without 
politeness or consideration. It describes something that is not diplomatic, and may 
be offensive. 
Ex) The comedian’s comment about the plane crash was completely tactless. I 
don’t think it’s right to joke about people’s deaths.  
connoisseur (n.) = a person with expert knowledge or very refined taste 
Ex) My father’s a wine connoisseur; he only buys the very best wines and he 
knows where to find them.  
reputable (adj.) = having a good reputation, worthy of trust 
 
Ex) I know a reputable used car salesman who will give you a fair price. 
dispute (v.) = argue, debate, or question the truth of something 
Ex) Scholars dispute the authenticity of these documents – some say they are 
truly from the twelfth century, and others believe they were written much later.

Complete Transcript 
Playing pranks on April 1st, which is known as April Fools’ Day, is a longstanding 
tradition in Western culture – and even companies and major media outlets get in 
on the act!  
In 1957, for example, the BBC aired a three-minute report about the unusually 
abundant spaghetti harvest in Switzerland – complete with footage of a Swiss 
family pulling strands of spaghetti off of tree branches. It may sound implausible 
that anyone would fall for such a story, but at the time, pasta was not an everyday 
food in Britain. It was sold in tins with tomato sauce and considered a delicacy, so 
much of the population was unaware that it did not, in fact, grow on trees. Hundreds 
of viewers called the station wanting to find out how to grow their own spaghetti 
trees. 
Another BBC April Fools’ prank duped a large number of listeners in 1977, when an 
astronomer announced that a once-in-a-lifetime planetary alignment would occur 
at precisely 9:47 AM. The planet Pluto would pass behind Jupiter, resulting in a 
temporary reduction in the force of the Earth's gravity. He told the audience that if 
they jumped in the air at the exact moment of the alignment, they would feel a 
strange floating sensation – and sure enough, one minute afterwards, listeners 
called in claiming they had jumped and floated around the room. 
One prank that did not go over too well was in 1933, when a newspaper published 
a doctored photo showing the collapse of the state capitol building after mysterious 
explosions. The accompanying article attributed the blasts to the accumulation of 
hot air generated by the politicians’ verbose debates. Although the end of the article 
indicated that it was a joke, readers were very upset and many wrote angry letters 
calling the prank tactless. 


If you think people aren't as easily fooled in more modern times, think again. In 
1998, Burger King placed a full-page ad in a newspaper announcing a revolutionary 
new hamburger. It was supposedly designed for left-handed burger connoisseurs in 
that all the condiments were rotated 180 degrees. The next day, thousands of lefties 
lined up to order the special sandwich, as right-handed people took pains to specify 
the correct orientation of their hamburger.  
The origin of April Fools’ Day dates back to the Roman Empire, when a court jester 
commented, within earshot of the emperor, that fools would make better rulers. 
The emperor, amused by the prospect, decreed that on one day of the year (April 
1st) a fool would preside over the Roman Empire - and thus the tradition was born. 
...or was it? In fact, the ancient Roman origin of April Fools’ Day is nothing more than 
a prank itself! A professor gave this explanation to a journalist in 1983, and it was 
reported in many reputable news outlets. The story was broadcast far and wide, yet 
nobody disputed it - and after a few weeks, the professor came forward and 
admitted that the whole thing was a hoax. The actual origins of April Fools’ Day are 
unknown, but the earliest association between April 1st and trickery is found in the 
literary classic Canterbury Tales, which was written in 1392. 
 
Extra Vocabulary 
"the BBC aired a three-minute report" 
 
To “air” something is to put it on television. 
"strands of spaghetti" 
The word “strand” describes something in the shape of a rope. It is often used 
for pieces of hair: “strands of hair.” 


"a newspaper published a doctored photo" 
A “doctored” photo is one that has been artificially modified, so it does not 
show a true representation of things. 
"The accompanying article attributed the blasts to the accumulation of hot air 
generated by the politicians’ verbose debates." 
The expression “hot air” in English means empty and exaggerated talk. If you 
say someone is “full of hot air,” it means they talk a lot, but there is not much 
real content or action. So the newspaper article was imagining that the “hot 
air” of the politicians actually caused an explosion   
"right-handed people took pains to specify the correct orientation of their 
hamburger" 
If you “take pains” to do something, it means you try hard to do it, or you 
make sure to do it deliberately. 
"a court jester commented, within earshot of the emperor, that fools would make 
better rulers" 
“Court jesters” were people whose job was to entertain and provide comedy 
for the rulers (kings, emperors, etc.) If you say something “within earshot” of 
someone else, it means you speak at a distance where the person can hear 
you. 
"the professor came forward and admitted that the whole thing was a hoax" 
The phrasal verb “come forward” is used for presenting yourself voluntarily. 
The word “hoax” is similar to a prank – the difference is that hoaxes are 
always intended to deceive (often to deceive a large number of people) 
whereas pranks are usually more mischievous and silly.
