Activity 1 – Pre-Learn Vocabulary 
rambunctious (adj.) = full of energy and disorderly (often used to describe kids) 
Ex) It’s hard to focus on reading a book when you have a rambunctious five-
year-old in the house.  
semblance (n.) = an appearance 
Ex) She tried to present some semblance of regret, but I could tell that she 
wasn’t really sorry for what she did. 
bluff (n./v.) = when you pretend confidently that you are going to do something, in 
order to trick another person into reacting in a certain way (this word comes from 
card games, when people try to deceive their opponents by acting as if they have 
better cards than they really do) 
Ex) The enemy claims they have a nuclear bomb, but we believe they’re bluffing 
in order to force us to give in to their demands. 
disruptive (adj.) = causing disturbance to the normal status 
Ex) There’s construction work being done on the building next door to our 
office, and the noise is really disruptive to our concentration. 
stunned (adj.) = filled with strong and sudden surprise (may be positive or 
negative) 
Ex) We were stunned when the six-year-old was able to multiply large numbers 
in her head. She’s a math genius! 


anticlimactic (adj.) = describes something (usually a final result or what should be 
a high point) that is disappointing when compared with the expectations for it 
Ex) The end of the movie was anticlimactic. The mystery was so intriguing, but 
the solution to it wasn’t very satisfying. 
obsessed (adj.) = if someone is “obsessed with” something, it means that thing 
occupies their thoughts excessively 
Ex) My teenage daughter is obsessed with boys. Sometimes it seems like getting 
a boyfriend is the only thing she talks about!

Complete Transcript 
I once volunteered to give martial arts classes to kids at a public school in a poor 
neighborhood. I had about twenty students ages six to nine, and I know kids that age 
are naturally rambunctious, but these kids were just completely out of control. If I 
looked away for two seconds, they would be climbing onto tables, hitting each 
other, knocking over chairs, and otherwise doing everything except for the activity I 
had asked them to do.  
It seemed like there was nothing I could do to maintain discipline or even any 
semblance of order whatsoever. I tried being nice, and the kids just ignored me. I 
tried being firmer and stricter, and that would work for a few minutes but then they 
would start bouncing off the walls again. Even with another teacher helping me, we 
just could not control the class. I even had to threaten to cancel it if they didn’t 
behave – and then I had to follow through on that so they could see I wasn’t 
bluffing. 
Well, after a lot of trial and error, I found that the best activities for holding their 
attention were ones that involved everybody working together as a group. One day 
we were doing a game that involved three teams, and I came up with a points 
system where the teams could gain points for following instructions and doing the 
movements I asked for, or lose points if they disobeyed or were disruptive. I asked 
the other teacher to keep score on a piece of paper, and so many points were won 
and lost that by the time the game finished I had no clue which team was in the lead. 
The other teacher gave me the scorecard, and when I tallied up all the points I saw 
that all three teams had tied with eight points each. I realized I’d need a tiebreaker, 
but I had no time to think of one – all the kids were looking at me and waiting for me 
to announce the results. So I said that team 1 had eight points, team 2 had eight 
points, and team 3 also had eight points.  


Well, the room just exploded in joy. The kids all started jumping up and down and 
hugging and congratulating each other. There was a huge smile on every face. I was 
stunned by their reaction. I had been expecting them to be kinda disappointed since 
the result was pretty anticlimactic. I mean, I had really played up the whole 
“competition” aspect, and in the end there was no clear winner. But the kids 
showed me that maybe it’s more important to achieve things all together than to be 
obsessed with winning. 
 
Extra Vocabulary 
"they would start bouncing off the walls again" 
The expression “bouncing off the walls” is used to describe it when kids are 
running around and acting crazy in an indoor space. 
"I had no clue which team was in the lead." 
 
“I had no clue” means “I had absolutely no idea.” 
"when I tallied up all the points" 
The phrasal verb “tally up” is another way to say “add up” various numbers to 
discover the final total. 
"I realized I’d need a tiebreaker" 
A “tiebreaker” is an extra activity that can break a tie (such as 1-1 or 3-3) and 
determine the winner and loser. 
"I had really played up the whole “competition” aspect" 
 
The phrasal verb “played up” means “emphasized.”
