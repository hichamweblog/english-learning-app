Lesson 43: Utopia 
 
Warm-Up: 
The word “utopia” refers to a society that is 
perfect politically, socially, economically, 
and culturally. If you could design a 
“perfect” society, what would it be like? 
 
 
 
Activity 1 – Pre-Learn Vocabulary 
flawless (adj.) = perfect, without errors, mistakes, or problems 
 
Ex) Her piano performance was flawless – she didn’t play a single wrong note. 
virtue (n.) = a morally excellent quality 
Ex) Sam never gives up when there’s a problem to be solved. Persistence is one 
of his strongest virtues.  
tyrant (n.) = an oppressive ruler who exercises power in a cruel manner 
Ex) The tyrant ordered his entire family to be killed so that nobody could 
challenge his claim to power. 
hereditary (adj.) = passing from parents to children 
Ex) I have a chronic illness, but luckily it’s not hereditary – so I don’t have to 
worry about passing it on to my future kids.  
destabilize (v.) = make something unstable, disturb it from its state of balance 
 
Ex) If anything destabilizes the nuclear reactor, there’s a risk of explosion. 
able-bodied (adj.) = people in good health, without any disabilities 


Ex) It makes me mad when I see able-bodied people park their cars in the 
handicapped parking spots. 
inevitable (adj.) = cannot be avoided 
 
Ex) Making mistakes is inevitable when you’re learning something new. 
brainwash (v.) = do psychological programming/manipulation so that a person 
adopts a specific set of beliefs 
Ex) The people in that religious cult are brainwashed into believing the end of 
the world is near.  
squash (v.) = suppress, eliminate 
Ex) I wanted to be a doctor, but my parents squashed my ambition by 
constantly telling me I wasn’t smart enough. 
sinister (adj.) = suggesting or threatening evil 
Ex) Jane’s ex-husband has sent her several sinister notes, and she’s afraid he 
might turn violent. 
overthrow (v.) = when the citizens of a society remove the existing 
rulers/authorities from power 
Ex) After ten years of fighting, the population finally managed to overthrow 
the dictator and establish a new government.

Complete Transcript 
The term “utopia” is a play on words – derived from Greek, it can mean both “a good 
place” as well as “no place” – perhaps implying that a completely flawless society is 
impossible to attain. 
The first documented description of a utopia is from Plato’s Republic, written in 380 
B.C. Plato, a Greek philosopher, described a society in which the people were 
organized into four socioeconomic classes.  
The “golden class” would be the rulers and decision-makers, possessing great 
wisdom and receiving training to become “philosopher-kings” who would ensure 
the fair distribution of resources. The main characteristic of the “silver class” would 
be courage, as they would be soldiers responsible for defending the state as well as 
enforcing laws. The “bronze and iron” classes would be the producers, with the 
primary virtue of moderation, so as to work for the good of the collective rather 
than accumulating individual wealth. 
Plato argued that the union of these three virtues – wisdom, courage, and 
moderation – would result in the ideal society. To prevent the members of the gold 
and silver classes from becoming tyrants, they would not be allowed to own private 
property; instead, they would be entirely dependent on the bronze and iron classes 
for their sustenance. Also, membership in the classes would not be hereditary; each 
child would be evaluated by the state and assigned according to their character and 
skills. 
In Plato’s utopia, artists and poets were prohibited by law, as he believed that the 
introduction of new ideas could destabilize society. Marriage and family would not 
exist; instead, sexual relations would be completely free and children would not 
know who their parents were. 
Sir Thomas More, writing fifteen centuries later, offers a different angle on utopia. In 
his version, there is no private ownership whatsoever; goods are kept in 


warehouses and people request what they need. Equality and uniformity are central 
themes – clothes and houses are simple, and the use of cosmetics and jewelry is 
discouraged. Meals are eaten in community, and everyone receives the same food. 
In More’s utopia, there are no lawyers because the laws of society are simple enough 
for everyone to understand. Agriculture is the primary profession, and every able-
bodied person must work six hours per day. Each household has two slaves – the 
slaves being criminals or foreigners captured in wars. Although the citizens of 
More’s utopia are pacifists, they recognize that war is sometimes inevitable – but 
they try to capture rather than kill their enemies. 
More radically differs from Plato in that he holds marriage in extremely high 
esteem, with punishments for having sex outside of marriage. Divorce is permitted 
only in very special circumstances, and considerable priority is placed on the 
educated selection of a lifelong mate. 
While some writers imagine utopian societies, others speculate about dystopias – 
societies containing fundamental and frightening flaws. Dystopias are often 
invented to highlight current, real-world issues which, if left unchecked, could 
develop into nightmares for humanity. 
Interestingly, the strict laws and social structures found in utopias also figure 
prominently in many dystopias. In Aldous Huxley’s novel Brave New World, children 
are produced artificially through cloning, and the population is genetically 
engineered to produce different social classes. Each person is then brainwashed 
from birth to fulfill their specific role in society, and any hint of individuality is 
squashed.  
Whereas in most utopias the role of science and technology is positive, Huxley 
shows us a sinister side to scientific progress. Modern movies like The Matrix are 
also dystopias, projecting the possible dangers of technology and the power of mass 
psychological manipulation. 
Almost all dystopias share one common feature – a group of rebels or members of a 
secret society who are not under the control of the system, and often work actively 
to overthrow it. This reflects the truth that no matter how heavy-handed the 
oppression, the human spirit is unconquerable. 


Extra Vocabulary 
“they would be entirely dependent on the bronze and iron classes for their 
sustenance” 
The word “sustenance” means supplies that maintain health and life 
(especially food) 
"Sir Thomas More, writing fifteen centuries later, offers a different angle on utopia." 
 
In this context, the word “angle” means a perspective or opinion 
"Dystopias are often invented to highlight current, real-world issues which, if left 
unchecked, could develop into nightmares for humanity." 
The verb “highlight” means “call special attention to.” The expression “if left 
unchecked” means “if nothing is done to stop or change it.” 
"This reflects the truth that no matter how heavy-handed the oppression, the 
human spirit is unconquerable." 
The adjective “heavy-handed” means strong and oppressive, and the word 
“unconquerable” means impossible to conquer, overcome, or defeat.
